Change Orders Renovation Existing Conditions

How to Reduce Change Orders on Renovation Projects With Incomplete As-Built Drawings

Most change-order advice assumes the drawings describing the existing building are trustworthy. On a renovation, that assumption is usually the first thing that's wrong — and it's wrong in a way a standard document review, run the standard way, won't catch.

JR
Joe Rossi
Founding Principal
September 27, 2026·10 min read

How to reduce change orders is usually framed as a coordination problem between drawings — does the structural sheet agree with the mechanical sheet, does the spec agree with the plan. That framing works on new construction, where every sheet in the set was drawn for this project, by this design team, against a site everyone can see. On a renovation, one of the documents in that coordination check wasn't drawn for this project at all. It's the as-built set describing a building someone else designed, years or decades ago, and it's frequently the least reliable sheet in the package.

Why This Is a Different Problem Than Ordinary Coordination Risk

A standard document review checks whether the drawings agree with each other. That catches a duct that clashes with a beam, a dimension that doesn't match between plan and detail, a spec section that contradicts what's drawn. All of that still matters on a renovation. But it assumes every sheet in the set is an accurate description of something real, and the as-built drawings on a renovation are the one sheet in the package that was drawn against a building nobody on the current design team field-verified from scratch.

That distinction matters because the two failure modes resolve completely differently. A conflict between two drawn elements is a paper problem — someone catches it, someone revises a sheet, the fix costs a redline. A conflict between a drawing and the real, physical building usually isn't found until a wall is opened, a ceiling is dropped, or a slab is cored, and by then the fix is a field change, a schedule hit, and a change order, not a markup.

Worth knowing

A 2020 study in the Journal of Construction Engineering and Management analyzed 517 change orders across 27 building renovation projects and found that change orders driven by unforeseen existing conditions carried a significantly higher mean cost than change orders from other causes — the concrete trade generated the most of these change orders, while HVAC carried the highest cost impact per change order. That's the same underlying mechanism at work here: it's not that renovation generates more paperwork, it's that the paperwork is more likely to be wrong about something physical.

Where Incomplete As-Builts Actually Come From

"As-built" on a renovation set can describe wildly different levels of reliability, and the drawings rarely say which one you're looking at:

  • A recent, field-verified 3D laser scan or point-cloud survey of the actual existing conditions for this project — the most reliable source available.
  • As-builts inherited from a prior renovation or tenant fit-out, updated for the last known scope but not field-verified for anything that's happened since.
  • The original construction documents, treated as if they describe current conditions, with no update at all for any alteration made in the years or decades since the building was first built.
  • An assumed match — no survey referenced at all, with the design simply drawn as if the building matches what an earlier set says it should look like.

Every one of these gets labeled "as-built" on a cover sheet, and a reviewer or design team working quickly can treat all four the same way. They aren't the same. A building that's changed hands, gone through multiple tenant improvements, or had unpermitted work done between renovations accumulates gaps between what the drawings say and what's actually behind the wall — and none of that shows up until someone checks.

What a Standard Review Misses When As-Builts Are the Weak Point

A cross-discipline document review built for new construction is looking for internal disagreement — does this trade's drawing match that trade's drawing. Run that same review against a renovation set drawn on incomplete as-builts, and it will pass. The mechanical drawing can agree perfectly with the structural drawing, the spec can agree with the plan, and the whole set can still be wrong, because everyone agreed with each other about a building that isn't the one standing on the site.

Catching that requires asking a different question than a standard coordination check asks: not "do these drawings agree with each other," but "what is this design's exposure to the existing-conditions documentation being wrong." That means verifying, before trusting anything drawn against it:

  • The source and age of the existing-conditions survey — field-verified for this specific project, or inherited from an earlier round with no update since.
  • The capture method — a recent 3D scan is materially more reliable than a hand-measured field survey, which is more reliable than an assumed match to old drawings with no verification at all.
  • Structural and MEP capacity against the new program — framing, foundations, electrical service, and HVAC tonnage were sized for the building's original use, not the one going in now, and a change of occupancy routinely exceeds what's already there.
  • Whether the code analysis was run against confirmed existing conditions — a code path selected against wrong as-builts can understate what the project actually needs to do to comply.

A Sequence for Reducing Change Orders When As-Builts Are the Risk

  1. Ask how the existing conditions were captured before reviewing anything else. If the answer is "inherited from the last renovation" or "assumed to match the original drawings," treat every dimension and condition on the set as unverified until proven otherwise, not as a settled fact.
  2. Prioritize verification where the trade data above says the cost lands hardest. Concrete and structural conditions generate the most change orders tied to unforeseen conditions; HVAC carries the highest per-change-order cost. Field-verify those systems first if a full survey isn't practical everywhere.
  3. Check structural and MEP capacity against the new occupancy explicitly, not as an assumption baked into the design. Existing infrastructure sized for the building's original use is a common blind spot precisely because nothing on the drawings flags that it wasn't re-checked.
  4. Run the cross-discipline review on top of this, not instead of it. Everything that belongs in a document review checklist still applies to a renovation set — drawing-to-spec agreement, structural-to-MEP coordination, readiness for bid. The existing-conditions check is an added layer, not a substitute.
  5. Know that renovation change-order exposure runs structurally higher than new construction, which changes how much verification effort is worth spending upfront. Renovation projects carry a different change-order profile than new-build work for exactly this reason — an unverified as-built isn't a minor risk on a reuse project, it's the dominant one.

The industry-wide cost of this problem is well documented even without renovation-specific numbers: the Construction Industry Institute estimates construction firms spend roughly $15 billion annually on rework tied to inaccurate initial drawings and version-control failures between the design team and the field. Renovation work concentrates that exposure in one specific place — the existing-conditions documentation — which is exactly why it's the first thing worth checking, not the last.

Key takeaways

  • On a renovation, the as-built drawings describing the existing building are frequently the least reliable sheet in the set — and a standard coordination review, which only checks drawings against each other, won't catch that.
  • A conflict between two drawings is a paper fix; a conflict between a drawing and the actual building usually isn't found until a wall or ceiling is already open, which is why it becomes a change order instead of a redline.
  • A 2020 study of 517 change orders across 27 renovation projects found unforeseen-existing-conditions change orders cost significantly more on average than change orders from other causes.
  • Verify the source, age, and capture method of the existing-conditions survey before trusting anything drawn against it, and check structural/MEP capacity against the new occupancy explicitly.
  • The existing-conditions check is an added layer on top of standard cross-discipline coordination review, not a replacement for it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you reduce change orders on a renovation when the as-built drawings can't be fully trusted?

Start by establishing how the existing conditions were actually captured — a recent field-verified survey, inherited as-builts, or an unverified assumption — before treating anything on the set as fact. Prioritize field verification of structural and MEP systems, since those generate the most change orders and the highest per-change-order costs tied to unforeseen conditions, then run a standard cross-discipline coordination review on top of that.

Why do renovation projects generate more change orders than new construction?

New construction only has to coordinate drawings against each other. A renovation set also has to be checked against the physical building it's designed around, and that building's documented conditions are frequently outdated, inherited from an earlier project, or never field-verified at all. That second layer of risk is on top of, not instead of, ordinary cross-discipline coordination risk.

What's the difference between a drawing conflict and an existing-conditions conflict?

A drawing conflict — two trades disagreeing with each other — is caught on paper and fixed with a redline before construction starts. An existing-conditions conflict is a disagreement between the drawings and the real building, and it's usually not discovered until a wall, ceiling, or slab is opened during construction, which turns it into a field change order instead of a free correction.

Is a recent 3D scan worth the cost on a renovation project?

Given that unforeseen-condition change orders carry a documented higher average cost than other change-order types, a field-verified survey is generally cheap relative to the exposure it removes — especially on structural and MEP systems, which are the trades most associated with unforeseen-condition change orders and their cost impact. The right call depends on the building's alteration history and how much of it is genuinely undocumented.

Does a document review catch existing-conditions problems the same way it catches drawing coordination problems?

Not automatically. A review scoped only to check drawings against each other will pass a renovation set that's internally consistent but still wrong about the building. Catching existing-conditions risk requires explicitly verifying the source and reliability of the as-built documentation as a separate step, not assuming it's covered by standard coordination checking.
