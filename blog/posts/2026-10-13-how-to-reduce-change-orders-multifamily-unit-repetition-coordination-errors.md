!lede If you want to know how to reduce change orders on a multifamily project, start with the feature everyone treats as a strength: repetition. A handful of unit types, stacked and mirrored across floors and buildings, makes a set feel pre-coordinated. It isn't. Repetition doesn't remove coordination errors; it copies them. One conflict that survives into the bid set can show up in every unit that shares the plan, and a fix that would have been a single drawing revision in design becomes a change order priced across the whole stack.

## Why repetition changes the math on a multifamily set

On a one-off building, a coordination error is usually a single location problem: one clash, one RFI, one field fix. On multifamily, the same error is often a *type* problem. If the Type B unit has a plumbing wall that lands on a structural member, or a bathroom exhaust duct with no path to the shaft, that condition exists everywhere Type B appears.

Two things follow from that:

- **Discovery is late and sequential.** The first occurrence is typically found when trades reach that floor in the field. By then, the same condition has already been priced, procured, and in some cases fabricated for every other repeat.
- **Cost scales with the count, not the conflict.** The design fix is the same size whether it's caught at 100% CDs or on level 3. What changes is how many units, how much prefabricated material, and how many resequenced trades the fix touches.

This is the same mechanism covered in [why so many change orders trace back to the same handful of interface points](/blog/why-so-many-change-orders-same-handful-interface-points/), with a multiplier attached: the interface points that fail are predictable, and in multifamily they fail in bulk.

## Where the errors hide

The unit plan itself is usually the most-checked sheet in the set. The conflicts sit one layer out from it.

### 1. Where the unit stack meets the structure

Plumbing walls, chases, and soffits stack vertically through the building, while the structure below may transfer, shift, or change at a podium or garage level. A unit layout that is clean on its own sheet can land a stack on a beam, a post-tension zone, or a shear wall that the architectural plan doesn't show. This is the core failure pattern in [MEP/structural coordination conflicts in high-rise residential](/blog/mep-structural-conflicts-high-rise-residential-shaft-riser-stacking/), and it applies to mid-rise and garden-style product too, just with fewer floors to multiply across.

### 2. Shaft and riser sizing against what's drawn

Mechanical, plumbing, and electrical each draw their own riser. The architectural shaft has to hold all of them, plus clearances, plus fire-rated enclosure requirements. The shaft is typically sized once and repeated, so an undersized shaft isn't a one-floor problem. Check shaft dimensions against the *sum* of what each discipline shows passing through it.

### 3. Corridor ceilings

Corridors are where every unit's ductwork, sprinkler mains, plumbing branches, and electrical runs converge. A repeated corridor section with a ceiling height that works at the typical condition can fail at every beam drop and every trapeze-hung main. If the reflected ceiling plan and the MEP drawings disagree on available depth, that disagreement is a repeat condition.

### 4. Fire and acoustic assemblies

Unit demising walls and floor-ceiling assemblies carry rating and sound-transmission requirements. The wall type tagged on the plan has to match the wall type in the assembly schedule and the penetration details the MEP drawings imply. A rated assembly interrupted by an unprotected penetration is a code issue first and a change order second, repeated at every demising wall.

### 5. Spec and schedule conflicts that repeat

Finish schedules, door and hardware schedules, and unit-type tags are where a single inconsistent entry gets applied to every unit that references it. These are spec-versus-drawing problems, covered in more depth in [spec vs. drawing conflicts](/blog/spec-vs-drawing-conflicts/), and repetition turns each discrepancy into a bulk purchasing decision.

## A review approach built for repeated product

The goal is to find the error once, in design, instead of once per floor in the field. A review built for multifamily follows from that.

1. **Inventory the unit types and the typical floor.** Every unique plan, plus the typical floor and the transitions (podium, roof, amenity levels, ground-floor commercial), gets coordinated as its own set.
2. **Coordinate each type against the building, not just itself.** Overlay structural, mechanical, plumbing, electrical, and fire protection on each type's footprint, then on the floor below and above it.
3. **Trace every vertical system top to bottom.** Risers, stacks, exhaust, and fire-rated shafts get followed from roof to base, since that's where transfers and offsets break the repeat.
4. **Check the atypical floors hardest.** The first residential level above a podium, the top floor, and any floor where the plan changes are where the repeating pattern is interrupted, and interruptions are where coordination slips.
5. **Price the repeat.** For each finding, note how many units or floors share the condition. That count is the real exposure, and it's what turns a minor-looking note into a priority item. [Calculating change order exposure before you bid](/blog/change-order-math-calculating-real-exposure/) walks through how to frame that.

## Why a second set of eyes matters more with repetition

Repetition creates a specific blind spot: the team that drew a unit type sees it hundreds of times and stops seeing it. Each discipline coordinates its own piece of the typical floor, and the review that would catch the cross-discipline conflict is the one nobody owns. That is the gap an independent, cross-discipline read of the full set is built to close, as laid out in [catching design errors before construction in multifamily podium construction](/blog/catching-design-errors-before-construction-multifamily-podium/).

None of this is a knock on the design team. Repeated product is efficient to design precisely because it's repeated, and that efficiency is what makes a single missed conflict so portable. Catching it early protects the architect's drawings, the GC's schedule, and the owner's budget at once.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What causes most change orders on multifamily projects?

The most common drivers are coordination gaps between disciplines and between drawings and specifications: plumbing stacks landing on structure, undersized shafts, corridor ceiling conflicts, and inconsistent schedules. No single universal percentage is confirmed across the industry, so the practical approach is to look at where your own set's interfaces are weakest.

### Why do repeated unit plans make change orders worse?

A single coordination error in a unit type exists everywhere that type is used. If it's found in the field instead of in design, the correction applies to every repeat, and any material already ordered or fabricated for those repeats is affected too.

### When is the best time to review a multifamily set?

Before the set goes out for bid, when a conflict can be fixed with a revision or addendum instead of a priced change. A second check at permit or at the point of major revisions catches what the changes introduced. See [when to hire a drawing review consultant](/blog/when-to-hire-a-drawing-review-consultant/) for timing in more detail.

### Does BIM coordination already catch this?

It catches geometric clashes between modeled elements, which helps a lot. It doesn't check whether the drawings agree with the specifications, whether a rated assembly is respected, or whether what's modeled matches what's issued. See [what BIM clash detection catches and misses](/blog/bim-clash-detection-what-it-catches-and-misses/).
