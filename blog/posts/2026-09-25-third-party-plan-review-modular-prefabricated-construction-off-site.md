!lede A modular or prefabricated project already has a third-party plan review built into it, and that surprises owners who assume the term describes an optional, owner-side add-on the way it does on a conventional site-built job. On a modular project, a state-approved third-party inspection agency reviews the module manufacturer's plans and inspects the factory-built components as a condition of code compliance — it's mandated, not optional, and it happens before a module ever leaves the plant. What that mandated review doesn't do is check whether the module manufacturer's drawings and the site design team's construction documents agree with each other at the points where the two scopes meet. That's a different question, and on a modular or prefabricated job, it's the one most likely to go unasked.

## The third-party review modular construction already requires

Volumetric modular manufacturers submit plans to a state-approved third-party inspection agency — organizations like ICC-NTA or Intertek operate in this role — or directly to a state industrialized building program, depending on the jurisdiction. That agency reviews the manufacturer's plans, performs factory inspections during fabrication, certifies that the materials and components meet applicable code, and issues certification labels and state insignias confirming compliance. This structure exists because modular units are built off-site, sometimes in a different state entirely, and arrive in a condition that makes a traditional on-site inspection of what's inside the finished module impossible after the fact — the third-party agency is the mechanism that lets a local building official trust what they can no longer see once the module is closed up and shipped.

Regulatory approval on a modular project is split for the same reason: state modular programs, typically working with an accredited third-party agency, approve the factory-built module and check code compliance during fabrication, while the local jurisdiction's own inspectors handle the site side — foundation, utility connections, final assembly, and overall compliance once the modules arrive. Both halves are real code review. Neither one is scoped to check the other half's drawings against it.

## Where the split creates a coordination gap instead of closing one

The factory/site split is a reasonable way to regulate a building that's designed and built in two places by two different parties working from two different sets of drawings. It's also exactly the structure that produces cross-discipline conflicts on a conventional site-built job, just relocated to a different boundary. Instead of the boundary sitting between the structural engineer and the mechanical engineer on one drawing set, it sits between the module manufacturer's engineering — often done by an in-house or subcontracted engineer working to the manufacturer's own standard details — and the site design team's construction documents, which are supposed to show how the modules land on the foundation, connect to each other, and tie into utilities that terminate at the building's perimeter.

Coordination between factory-installed and site-installed scope has to be explicit, because it doesn't happen automatically just because both halves eventually get inspected. A fire sprinkler system is a common example: branch lines are often pre-installed inside the module at the factory, while the main that feeds them is installed on-site after the modules are set. If the module drawings and the site drawings don't agree on exactly where that connection point sits, at what elevation, and with what fitting, the mismatch doesn't surface until a crew is trying to make the connection in the field — after both halves have already been separately reviewed and separately certified as code-compliant, because each review only had visibility into its own half.

## Tolerances are where the gap shows up first

Construction tolerances are consistently one of the hardest coordination problems on a modular job, precisely because of the connections between modules. A module is fabricated to a fixed dimension in a factory, transported, and then has to land within a tight tolerance on a foundation and against adjacent modules that were fabricated separately — sometimes in a different production run, sometimes by a different crew. Architectural and structural drawings for a modular project need to carry dimensional tolerances specific to transport and stacking, not the tolerances a site-built structural connection would normally use, and increasingly rely on BIM coordination precisely because module interfaces require more precise up-front specification than field-adjustable, site-built connections typically do. Misaligned tolerances, missing documentation on how a connection is supposed to work, or an unverified assumption about how one module's edge condition meets the next module's edge condition are what turn into expensive field rework and, as importantly, into disputes about whether the manufacturer's design or the site design team's drawings were the ones that got it wrong.

That question — whose drawing was right — is exactly the kind of cross-discipline conflict [an independent review checks for that a single party's own QC process structurally can't](/blog/third-party-plan-review-scope-vs-architect-qc/), for the same reason a design team's internal review has never been well-positioned to catch a conflict between two different parties' drawings. On a modular project, the two parties aren't the architect and the MEP engineer on the same set — they're the manufacturer's engineering team and the site design team, working from different base documents, on different schedules, frequently without a shared model that shows both halves in the same coordinate space.

## Why the regulatory split doesn't get resolved the way permitting sequencing does

On a conventional project, permitting and design review at least run through a single AHJ that eventually sees the whole set, even if [the plan review and permitting processes intersect at different points along the way](/blog/third-party-plan-review-ahj-permitting-intersection/). On a modular project, that single point of convergence often doesn't exist in the same form — the state modular program and the local jurisdiction are reviewing different halves of the same building under different programs, and neither one is structured to catch a disagreement between the two halves, because neither one's mandate extends past its own half.

This isn't a flaw specific to modular delivery so much as a sharper version of a pattern that shows up whenever more than one party's drawings have to agree at a boundary neither party fully owns. It's the same underlying problem covered in [how design-assist trade partner input needs to be checked against the current state of adjacent disciplines, not just against itself](/blog/third-party-plan-review-design-assist-trade-partners-early/): early or off-site input from one party doesn't automatically stay coordinated with a second party's drawings just because both parties eventually get their own sign-off. On a design-assist job, the fix is checking the trade partner's input against what the rest of the design team drew after that input was given. On a modular job, the fix is the same idea applied to a harder version of the same gap: checking the manufacturer's module drawings against the site design team's drawings at every point the two scopes meet, because neither the state agency's factory review nor the local jurisdiction's site inspection is scoped to do that on its own.

## What an independent review adds on a modular or prefabricated project

An owner bringing in an independent, cross-discipline document review on a modular project isn't duplicating the state-approved agency's work or the local jurisdiction's inspections — it's covering the specific area neither of those reviews is scoped to touch:

- **Module-to-module connection details**, checked for whether adjacent modules' structural and enclosure drawings agree on tolerance, fastening method, and sequence.
- **Module-to-foundation interface**, checked against the site's structural and civil drawings for elevation, anchor layout, and tolerance stack-up across multiple modules landing on one foundation.
- **Factory-to-site utility handoffs** — plumbing, fire protection, electrical — checked for whether the termination point shown in the module drawings matches the connection point shown in the site drawings, in location, elevation, and fitting type.
- **Whether the two document sets were coordinated against a shared, current model**, rather than each independently coordinated against an earlier version of the other.

None of that is code-compliance review, and none of it duplicates what the state-approved third-party agency or the local jurisdiction already does. It's the coordination check that exists in the space between two mandated reviews that were never built to talk to each other.

Modular and prefabricated delivery genuinely reduces some categories of field risk by moving fabrication into a controlled factory environment with its own mandated inspection regime. It doesn't remove the need for a party checking whether that factory's drawings and the site's drawings agree with each other — if anything, splitting the project across two separately regulated environments makes that check more necessary, not less, because it's the one thing neither mandated review was ever scoped to do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Doesn't modular construction already have a third-party plan review, since it's required by code?

Yes, but that mandated review is scoped to the module manufacturer's factory-built component and administered by a state-approved agency for code compliance — it doesn't check whether the manufacturer's drawings agree with the site design team's drawings at the points where the two scopes connect. Those are two different functions that happen to share the phrase "third-party review."

### Who is responsible for checking that a module's drawings match the site's drawings?

Under the standard modular regulatory structure, nobody's mandate explicitly covers that check. The state-approved agency reviews the factory-built module against code; the local jurisdiction inspects the site work against its own code review. An independent, cross-discipline document review is what fills that specific gap.

### Why are tolerances a bigger issue on modular projects than on conventional site-built projects?

Because a module is fabricated to a fixed dimension off-site and then has to land within a tight tolerance against a foundation and adjacent modules it wasn't physically test-fit against beforehand. Site-built connections can often be field-adjusted; a factory-built module's dimensions are largely fixed by the time it arrives, so a tolerance mismatch is a rework problem rather than a field-adjustment problem.

### Does BIM coordination solve the module-to-site coordination gap on its own?

It helps, but only if both the manufacturer's model and the site design team's model are actually coordinated against each other in a shared coordinate space and kept current. A model existing for each half separately doesn't guarantee the two were checked against one another, which is the same gap an independent review is positioned to check directly.

### Should an owner request an independent review before or after modules start fabrication?

Before, if possible — checking the interface details on paper against the site design is far cheaper than discovering a tolerance or connection-point conflict after a module has already been built to a dimension that doesn't match the site condition it's arriving to meet.
