!lede On a public agency project, the choice between constructability review and peer review usually isn't made on merit alone. It is made by a procurement document: a design RFP, a capital program manual, or a funding condition that says a review will happen before bid. The risk is treating the box as checked and assuming the set is coordinated. Constructability review vs peer review is a question about what each one examines, and a procurement requirement tells you which one was asked for, not which one you need.

## What a procurement requirement usually asks for

Public owners write review requirements for accountability. They want a documented, independent check before money is committed to a bid. The language varies widely by agency, funding source, and delivery method, and we don't claim a universal standard. Read your own documents. But the requirement tends to take one of three forms:

- **A named review type.** The manual says "peer review," "constructability review," or "independent technical review," sometimes without defining it.
- **A milestone.** A review at a stated design percentage, such as schematic, design development, or 90% construction documents.
- **A qualification.** The reviewer must be independent of the design team, hold certain licenses, or carry certain insurance.

Each one can be met while leaving the most expensive question unasked. If the requirement only names a review type, the scope is whatever the reviewer's proposal says it is.

## What each review is built to examine

The general difference is covered in [constructability review vs. peer review: what's the actual difference](/blog/constructability-review-vs-peer-review/). On a public project, the practical split looks like this.

### Peer review

A peer review is typically a check by another design professional on the design's technical soundness: code compliance, calculations, design intent, and the standard of care. It answers, "Is this discipline's work correct?" That is what many agency manuals mean by the term, and it is a legitimate requirement for the design to meet.

### Constructability review

A constructability review looks at whether the set can be built as drawn, in the sequence the contract implies, by the trades who will build it. It asks about access, sequencing, interfaces, and what a bidder will have to interpret. It answers, "Can the contractor price and build this without guessing?"

## Where each one stops

The gap on public work is a specific one, and it is cross-discipline. A peer reviewer working inside one discipline can confirm that discipline is internally correct. The conflict that costs money tends to sit between two of them: a duct route through a beam, a fire-rated wall with more penetrations than its detail allows, a structural opening that doesn't match the mechanical riser. We go through why in [which one actually catches cross-discipline conflicts](/blog/constructability-review-vs-peer-review-cross-discipline-conflicts/).

Constructability review tends to be where those seams get checked, but it can have its own limits. A review that only reads drawings can miss a specification section that contradicts them. A review run at 50% documents can't see what the 100% set adds.

## Why public bidding raises the stakes

Under public bid rules, the owner can't negotiate scope quietly after award. Bidders price what is in the documents, the low responsive bid generally wins, and gaps become change orders afterward. We covered that dynamic in [change order risk mitigation on public bid projects](/blog/change-order-risk-mitigation-public-bid-low-bid-rules/). Addenda also have to reach every bidder, which is why the timing of a review matters; see [what changes when addenda have to go to every bidder](/blog/pre-bid-drawing-review-public-bid-addenda-every-bidder/).

The result is that a coordination error found before bid costs a sheet revision, and one found after award is a change order priced by a contractor who already holds the contract. That is the reason to decide the review's scope on purpose rather than by the manual's wording.

## How to scope the review to the requirement

1. **Read the requirement literally.** Note the named review type, the milestone, and any independence or qualification language. Don't assume the term means what it means in the private sector.
2. **Ask the agency what satisfies it.** Many agencies will confirm in writing whether an additional review meets or supplements the requirement. Get the answer before the RFP goes out.
3. **Write the scope into the review contract.** If cross-discipline coordination, drawing-to-spec conflicts, and sequencing are in scope, say so. If they aren't, know that they aren't.
4. **Time it before the bid set is final.** A review that lands after the addenda window can only produce more addenda.
5. **Keep the confidentiality and independence terms clean.** Agencies often require both. An NDA signed before any drawings move, and a reviewer with no stake in the design, handle both.

## Using both without duplicating work

The two reviews don't compete. A peer review confirms the design is right. A constructability review confirms the set holds together. If the agency requires a peer review, it can usually run alongside an independent coordination review, and the second review's findings, which are sheet-located, severity-ranked, and carry cost and schedule impact, give the design team something specific to correct before issue.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a peer review satisfy a public agency's constructability review requirement?

Not automatically. The two reviews examine different things, and agencies define the terms differently. Ask the agency in writing whether the peer review meets the requirement as it is worded in your documents.

### Can an independent constructability review be added to a required peer review?

Generally yes. Agencies commonly allow additional reviews beyond the minimum, and the two address different questions. Confirm that the added review doesn't conflict with any independence or confidentiality terms in the procurement documents.

### When should the review happen on a public bid project?

Before the bid set is final, while a correction is still a sheet revision rather than an addendum or a change order. Reviewing at a late stage still helps, but it leaves less room to correct without delaying bid.

### What should a review scope say to cover cross-discipline conflicts?

It should state that all disciplines and the specifications are reviewed together, and that findings are located by sheet and ranked by severity with cost and schedule impact. If the scope is silent on this, treat it as not covered.
