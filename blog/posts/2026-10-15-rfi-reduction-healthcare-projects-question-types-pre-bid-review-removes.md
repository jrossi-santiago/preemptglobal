!lede RFI reduction on a healthcare project doesn't mean driving the count to zero. It means removing the questions that exist only because two sheets, or a sheet and a spec section, disagree. A pre-bid review is aimed at that category, and on a hospital or outpatient set, that category is large.

## Sorting healthcare RFIs by where the question comes from

Every RFI is a question the set couldn't answer. The useful split isn't by trade or by urgency. It's by *why the set couldn't answer it*:

1. **Conflict questions.** The documents contradict each other, so the contractor can't price or build without a ruling.
2. **Omission questions.** Something needed to build the work isn't shown or specified anywhere.
3. **Clarification questions.** The information exists but is ambiguous, buried, or spread across sheets in a way that's easy to misread.
4. **Genuine design-decision questions.** The design isn't settled yet: equipment not selected, an owner decision still open, a field condition nobody could verify before opening walls.

A pre-bid review works on the first three. It reads the issued set the way a bidder and a field crew will, across every discipline at once, and flags what doesn't reconcile before the set is priced. The fourth category is a real part of healthcare work, and no document review makes it disappear. It does keep that category from being inflated by questions that never needed to exist.

## The question types a pre-bid review removes on a healthcare set

### Ceiling and above-ceiling coordination questions

Healthcare ceilings carry more than most building types: medical gas, large-diameter ductwork, sprinkler mains, cable tray, lighting, and in patient rooms, track and boom accommodation. When the reflected ceiling plan, the mechanical layout, and the structure above don't agree on available depth, the question reaches the field as "which one moves?" That is a conflict question. A review that overlays those sheets before bid turns it into an addendum item instead of an RFI with a schedule clock attached.

### Medical gas and plumbing routing questions

Medical gas lines, plumbing risers, and the architectural wall types they run through are drawn by different people on different sheets. Typical conflict questions include a riser shown through a rated shaft wall that has no room for it, a gas outlet location that differs between the architectural room sheet and the piping plan, and a valve box location that lands in a wall type that can't hold it. Each is findable by comparing sheets. None requires a design decision once the discrepancy is on the table.

### Room data sheet versus drawing questions

Healthcare sets lean on room data sheets or room finish and equipment schedules to carry requirements: pressure relationships, air change expectations, outlet counts, finishes. When a schedule says one thing and the plan or mechanical sheet says another, the contractor asks. We covered the equipment-and-schedule version of this in [spec vs. drawing conflicts on healthcare projects](/blog/spec-vs-drawing-conflicts-healthcare-equipment-room-data-sheets/). Many of those RFIs are conflict questions in a different costume, and a cross-check removes them at the source.

### Fire and smoke rating continuity questions

Rated barriers have to be continuous on the drawings before they can be continuous in the building. RFIs here usually read "wall type shown stops at the ceiling, is it rated to deck?" or "penetration detail doesn't match the rated assembly." These are omission and clarification questions, and a reviewer reading architectural life-safety sheets against MEP penetrations will find the gaps before a subcontractor does.

### Structural opening and support questions

Rooftop and mechanical-room equipment, shafts, and major penetrations need openings and supports shown in the structural set. When the mechanical drawings call for an opening the structure doesn't show, or a unit whose weight and support aren't carried through, the question lands as an RFI mid-framing. This is the same pattern we describe in [where structural and MEP conflicts originate](/blog/structural-vs-mep-where-coordination-conflicts-originate/): two disciplines each drew their half, and nobody compared them.

### Spec section versus drawing note questions

Division-by-division, healthcare specs are dense. A drawing note that references a product or standard the spec section doesn't mention, or a spec requirement the drawings never show, produces a steady stream of "which governs?" RFIs. Reviewing specs and drawings together, not one after the other, is what catches them.

## The question types a pre-bid review does not remove

Being straight about this matters, because overselling RFI reduction is how it loses credibility with project teams.

- **Unfinalized equipment.** If a major piece of medical equipment hasn't been selected when the set is issued, the questions about its footprint, power, and structure will come later. A review can flag that the set is carrying an assumption. It can't make the selection.
- **Existing conditions in renovation work.** Healthcare projects frequently happen inside operating facilities. Concealed conditions behind existing walls and above existing ceilings generate questions no drawing review can answer.
- **Owner and clinical decisions still open.** Room use changes and operational preferences resolved during construction produce RFIs that aren't document errors.
- **Phasing and infection control specifics** that depend on the facility's operations rather than on the drawings.

For that reason, we'd frame the goal as getting RFIs to be about the things that are actually undecided, not about things the set got wrong. It's the same argument made in [RFI volume isn't a KPI](/blog/rfi-volume-not-a-kpi-what-to-track-instead/): the count matters less than what's in it.

## What the review produces

The output is a written findings report. Every conflict is located by sheet, ranked by severity, and carries a dollar exposure and schedule impact per item. For RFI reduction purposes, the useful move is to read the report as a list of RFIs that haven't been written yet. Each finding that's resolved by addendum before bid is one fewer question a bidder has to price around, and one fewer contractor-to-architect round trip during construction.

That also keeps the architect's and GC's jobs easier rather than harder. A cleaner set means fewer interruptions for the design team during construction administration and fewer unpriced unknowns for the GC and subs. Everyone in the chain spends less time on questions that shouldn't have existed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a pre-bid review eliminate RFIs on a healthcare project?

No, and it shouldn't claim to. It removes the RFIs caused by conflicts, omissions, and ambiguity inside the issued set. Questions that stem from open design decisions, unselected equipment, or concealed existing conditions will still come up, but they become a larger share of a smaller list.

### Which healthcare RFIs are the most preventable?

The ones that come from cross-discipline conflicts: ceiling space shared by ductwork, sprinkler, and medical gas; risers and routing through rated walls; schedules that disagree with plans; and structural openings the mechanical set requires but the structural set omits. Each can be found by comparing sheets, with no design decision needed.

### Why do healthcare sets generate so many coordination RFIs?

They pack more systems into the same building volume than most project types, and the requirements for those systems are spread across drawings, room data sheets, and dense specifications. More interfaces between disciplines means more places where one document can disagree with another.

### When in the project should the review happen?

Before the set is priced, so conflicts are corrected by addendum instead of surfacing as RFIs and change orders after award. See our [guide to pre-bid versus IFC timing](/blog/pre-bid-drawing-review-vs-ifc-timing/) for how timing changes what a fix costs.

### Does reducing RFIs mean the architect did something wrong?

No. Multi-discipline sets are assembled by many hands under tight schedules, and cross-discipline gaps are a normal byproduct. An independent review exists to catch what coordination between those hands can miss, and it helps the design team as much as the contractor.
