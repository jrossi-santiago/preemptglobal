!lede Owner's representative drawing review on a higher education capital program is a different job from reviewing one building. A university rarely builds a single structure: it funds a residence hall, a science building, and a central plant upgrade under one bond, one board, and one calendar. Each building arrives as its own set, often from its own design team, and the owner's rep is the person responsible for all of them. The review has to read each set on its own and then read the sets against each other.

## Why a multi-building program changes the review

On a single project, the question is whether the architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and fire protection sheets agree. On a campus program, that question repeats for every building, and a second one appears: do the buildings agree with the shared infrastructure and with each other?

Three things make that second question hard:

- **Different design teams, different standards.** One firm detailed the lab building, another the residence hall, a third the utility work. Each team's drawing conventions, sheet organization, and specification editing habits differ, even when the university's design standards are the same.
- **Different schedules.** Buildings in a program rarely reach 100% construction documents on the same day. The set that goes out first sets assumptions the later sets have to live with.
- **Shared systems.** Chilled water, steam or hot water, electrical service, telecommunications, storm drainage, and fire access are usually designed as campus systems, then tapped by each building.

The result is that a coordination conflict can sit between two sets that were each internally clean. The baseline cross-discipline work is covered in [what an independent review picks up where architect QC leaves off](/blog/architect-qc-vs-owner-review/). A program adds the layer between buildings.

## Where conflicts hide between buildings

### Campus utility tie-ins

The central plant or utility sheet shows capacity, pipe sizes, and connection points. Each building's mechanical and electrical sheets show the load they expect to draw. When those two sources disagree, the discrepancy surfaces during commissioning, when someone finds the building was sized for a service that was never provided. A program-level review compares the load assumptions on the building sheets against what the utility set delivers at the connection point.

### Site and civil handoffs

Grading, storm routing, and temporary construction access cross building boundaries. A site plan in one set can show an area as laydown while a neighboring set shows new paving or utility work in the same spot. Fire lane access, pedestrian routes, and construction entrances need to hold for every phase, not just the one being drawn.

### Specifications that drift apart

Owners often issue a master specification or design standard for the program. Over time, individual design teams edit sections for their building. A review that reads two specification books side by side finds the same section calling for different products, submittals, or testing. That creates bid-day confusion when the same trades price more than one building. The patterns are the ones in [spec vs. drawing conflicts: where the two disagree](/blog/spec-vs-drawing-conflicts/), with an extra axis: spec against spec across buildings.

### Phasing and swing space

Higher education programs usually have to keep classes running. Demolition of one building may depend on the occupants being moved into another building that has to be finished first. That sequence appears on phasing sheets, in specification front-end sections, and on the schedule, and all three need to say the same thing. [Owner's rep review of multi-phase programs](/blog/owners-representative-drawing-review-multi-phase-programs/) goes deeper on drift between phases.

## How to scope the review for a program

An owner's rep can scope this several ways. A workable structure has three parts.

1. **A full pass on every building.** Drawings and specifications, all disciplines, reviewed together. This is the same standard as any single-building review.
2. **A shared-systems pass.** One reviewer reads the campus utility, site, and infrastructure sets against the building sets that connect to them, tracing each tie-in from source to point of use.
3. **A carry-forward log.** Findings from the first building to go out are checked against the later ones. If the lab building had a recurring detail problem, the next building probably has it too, and the log catches it before that set is issued.

The third piece is where a program earns the most from a single reviewing team. A reviewer who read the first set already knows which sheets to open first on the second.

## Timing across a staggered program

Because buildings reach bid at different times, the review is a rolling series, not one event. The practical rule is the same as for any set: review before the documents go out, when a fix is a revised sheet and not an addendum or a change order. For a program, add a second rule: review the earliest set first, because its assumptions travel. The same logic is at work in [what to ask before hiring a drawing review firm](/blog/owners-representative-drawing-review-hiring-questions/), where continuity of reviewer across sets is one of the questions worth asking. For a single higher education building that mixes labs and classrooms, [the document review checklist for lab and classroom hybrids](/blog/document-review-checklist-higher-education-lab-classroom-hybrid/) is a good companion.

A 48-hour turnaround per review pass matters here. On a staggered program, a reviewer who returns findings quickly lets the design team close them before the next building's set is due, so the lessons carry forward while they still matter.

## What the owner's rep gets out of it

The deliverable is a written findings report for each set: every conflict located by sheet, ranked by severity, with cost exposure and schedule impact for each item. For the program, the rep also gets the cross-building view, which is the part no single design team can produce. It gives the rep specific, sheet-located items to bring to each design team, framed as clarifications to the documents, not as criticism. The design teams get a faster path to a clean set, the contractors get clearer bid documents, and the university gets fewer surprises on a calendar tied to the academic year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does an owner's representative drawing review cover on a university program?

It covers drawings and specifications across all disciplines for each building, plus the connections between buildings: campus utilities, site and access plans, and phasing. The output is a written report for each set with sheet-located findings.

### Should each building be reviewed separately or together?

Both. Each building needs its own full pass, and a shared-systems pass reads the buildings against the campus infrastructure and against each other. Reviewing them in isolation misses conflicts that sit between sets.

### When in the program schedule should the review happen?

Before each set is issued for bid, starting with the first building to go out. Its assumptions carry into later sets, so catching problems early prevents them from repeating.

### Does a program-level review replace the design team's QC?

No. Each design team checks its own building against its own standards. The review adds an outside read of the whole set and of the lines between buildings, which no individual team is assigned to cover.
