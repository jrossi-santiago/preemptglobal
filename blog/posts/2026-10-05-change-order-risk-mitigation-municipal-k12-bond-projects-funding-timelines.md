!lede Change order risk mitigation looks different on a municipal or K-12 bond project, because the money is fixed before the design is done. Voters approved a number. A board adopted a schedule. The district or city has no easy way to go back for more, so a change order that a private owner would absorb as an irritation becomes a decision about what gets cut from the building.

## Why bond funding changes the risk

On a privately funded project, a change order is a cost and a schedule problem. On a bond project it is also a governance problem. Three features of public funding make the exposure sharper.

- **The budget is set early and publicly.** The bond amount was often established when the program was only a list of needs. By the time documents reach bid, the number has not moved, and the design has had to fit into it.
- **Contingency is thin and visible.** Contingency has to cover unforeseen conditions, and it also ends up covering every coordination conflict that reached the field. When it runs out, the board sees it in a public meeting.
- **Deadlines are tied to something outside the project.** A K-12 building has to be ready when school starts. A municipal facility may be tied to a grant window, a lease expiration, or a bond expenditure deadline. A schedule slip is not just a delay; it can be a missed opening.

None of this is a criticism of the design team or the contractor. It is a description of the box the project sits in. The goal of mitigation is to put as many problems as possible on paper, where they cost a revised sheet, before they reach the field, where they cost a change order.

## Where the exposure builds on a bond project

### Early budgets and late documents

Bond budgets are frequently set from programming-level information. The drawings that arrive at bid are far more detailed, and gaps between what the budget assumed and what the documents show turn up as scope. When a coordination conflict is found after award, the fix is priced against a budget that has no room left for it. A pre-bid check is the cheapest point to find it, as covered in [why change order risk mitigation starts before the bid](/blog/change-order-risk-mitigation-starts-before-bid/).

### Low-bid rules and thin margins

Most public bodies award to the lowest responsive bidder. That structure rewards tight pricing, and a contractor who priced the documents as drawn has little cushion for conflicts discovered later. Those conflicts become change order requests. [Change order risk mitigation on public bid projects](/blog/change-order-risk-mitigation-public-bid-low-bid-rules/) covers how the low-bid mechanics interact with this.

### Compressed summer windows

K-12 work often has to be done between the end of one school year and the start of the next. That window leaves no float for rework. A MEP and structural conflict that would be a few days of reroute on a normal schedule can push completion past the first day of class. The interface points that fail most often are the same ones that show up elsewhere, as described in [the handful of interface points behind most change orders](/blog/why-so-many-change-orders-same-handful-interface-points/), but the schedule consequence is larger.

### Occupied buildings and phased work

Municipal and school renovations frequently happen with people in the building. Phasing sheets, temporary egress, and utility shutdowns all have to match across drawings and specifications. Where they don't, the resolution happens in the field, under pressure, with the owner's staff in the way.

### Multiple approvals

Bond projects often need sign-off from a board, a state education or facilities agency, and a building department. A change order may require a formal approval step that takes longer than the work itself. Avoiding it is better than processing it.

## What a review should check on a bond-funded set

A document review before bid is most useful here when it is aimed at the places where bond projects tend to run out of room. A practical focus list:

1. **Scope against the budget assumptions.** Items shown in the drawings that the specifications do not cover, or the reverse, are the ones that turn into "not in the base bid" discussions.
2. **Cross-discipline conflicts.** Structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and fire protection sheets read against each other for clearances, penetrations, and routing.
3. **Phasing and occupancy.** Egress, shutdown sequences, and temporary conditions checked across the phasing plan, the specifications, and the discipline sheets.
4. **Addenda consistency.** Revisions issued during bidding can fix one sheet and leave another contradicting it. A final read after the last addendum is worth the time.
5. **Agency-driven requirements.** State or local requirements that appear in the specifications but not on the drawings, or vice versa.

The review output that matters for a public owner is a findings report: every conflict located by sheet, ranked by severity, with the cost exposure and schedule impact of each. A board or facilities director can act on that. It turns "we think the set is probably fine" into a list of specific items the design team can clarify before bid.

## Timing the review against the funding calendar

For a bond project, the review has to fit inside the procurement calendar rather than sit beside it. The useful moment is after the documents are substantially complete and before they are issued for bid, because a finding then is a revised sheet. After award it is a change order that needs approval. A 48-hour turnaround per review pass keeps the check inside the typical window between final design and advertisement, without moving the bid date.

It also helps to think about who reads the report. Bond-funded projects have a design team, a construction manager or owner's representative, and a board or oversight committee. A written report with sheet-located findings gives each of them the same facts, so the conversation is about fixes. The same logic applies to estimating, where [the exposure framework for design errors and omissions](/blog/cost-of-design-errors-and-omissions-exposure-framework/) helps an owner put a range on what an unreviewed set could cost.

## Framing it for the design team

A review is not an accusation. Every design team on a public project is working against the same fixed budget and the same deadline, and most would rather hear about a conflict on a drawing than in a field RFI. The tone that works is clarification: here is a sheet reference, here is what reads differently from the specification, please confirm which governs. That keeps the team's time on the fix. The contractor benefits too, because bidders price a clearer set with less contingency padding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are change orders harder to manage on bond-funded projects?

The budget was set by a public vote or board action, and contingency is limited. Change orders may also need formal approval, which adds time. The combination leaves little room to absorb conflicts found in the field.

### When should a municipal or school district review its documents?

After the set is substantially complete and before it is issued for bid. At that point a conflict is fixed with a revised sheet. After award, the same conflict is typically a priced change order that needs approval.

### Does an independent review replace the architect's quality control?

No. The design team checks its own work against its own standards. An independent review is an outside read of the full drawing and specification set across all disciplines, aimed at the conflicts that sit between them. It works alongside the design team's QC.

### What should a review of a school or municipal set focus on?

Cross-discipline conflicts, scope gaps between drawings and specifications, phasing and egress for occupied buildings, and consistency after the last addendum. Those are the areas where a fixed budget and a fixed opening date leave the least room for error.
