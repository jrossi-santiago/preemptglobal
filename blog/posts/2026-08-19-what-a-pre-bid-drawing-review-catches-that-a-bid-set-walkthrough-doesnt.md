!lede Most owners assume the pre-bid job walk and a pre-bid drawing review are doing roughly the same job at roughly the same stage — two forms of due diligence before award, redundant with each other if a project already has one. They aren't redundant, because they aren't checking the same thing. A walkthrough is a site visit: contractors verify access, laydown space, existing utilities, and site conditions against what the drawings show. A pre-bid drawing review is a document-level read: every discipline's sheets checked against every other discipline's sheets, and the specs checked against the drawings, for the contradictions that don't show up on a site tour at all because they don't live on the site — they live in the paper.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should, because the bid window is the cheapest point in the entire project to fix a cross-discipline conflict, and a walkthrough was never designed to find one.

## What a bid-set walkthrough actually does

A pre-bid job walk exists to answer physical-condition questions: what does the site actually look like, where can equipment stage, what utilities are already in the ground, what access constraints will affect means and methods. Contractors use the walk to verify what they can see against what the drawings claim, and to ask clarifying questions about scope — instructions to bidders typically spell out the walkthrough schedule alongside the deadline for submitting formal RFIs, because the walk and the RFI process are meant to work together as one track of due diligence.

None of that is a criticism of the walkthrough. It's doing exactly what it's built to do. The problem is what it isn't built to do: nobody on a job walk is holding the structural sheet next to the mechanical sheet and tracing every duct run against every beam location. That's not a site question — it's a document question, and it requires sitting with the full set, not standing on the lot.

## What a bid-set gets skimmed for, not read for

The other piece of due diligence that happens before bid is the estimator's own read of the set — but that read has a different job too. An estimating team is extracting quantities and scope to build a number, working sheet by sheet against a deadline, not cross-referencing every discipline against every other discipline for conflicts that don't affect the takeoff either way. A dimension that disagrees between the architectural and structural sheets, or a spec section that calls for a fire rating the drawn detail doesn't accommodate, can sit in a set that's been walked, estimated, and priced by multiple bidders without anyone catching it — because catching it was never anyone's job in that process. [Seven of the most common ways this shows up are covered in our document review checklist](/blog/bid-set-red-flags-document-review-checklist/); the pattern across all of them is the same: the conflict was visible on paper the entire time, just never read for on purpose.

## The three things a pre-bid drawing review catches that neither one does

**Cross-discipline conflicts that only exist between two sheets.** A structural beam and a mechanical duct routed through the same space aren't wrong on their own drawings — each sheet, read in isolation, looks complete and correct. The conflict only exists in the overlap, which means it's invisible to anyone reading one discipline's sheets at a time, whether that's an estimator pricing structural steel or a mechanical sub pricing ductwork. A drawing review is built specifically to hold every discipline against every other discipline, sheet by sheet, which is the only way that category of conflict gets caught before it's priced.

**Spec-versus-drawing contradictions.** The specifications and the drawings are two separate documents describing the same building, produced on separate tracks, and they don't always agree — a spec section can call for a clearance, rating, or material that the drawn detail doesn't actually accommodate. Neither document is wrong read by itself. A walkthrough never touches the specs at all, and an estimator's read of the specs is scoped to pricing, not to checking them line-by-line against the drawn conditions.

**Dangling or renumbered references.** Every set is full of small circled callouts pointing to a detail on another sheet — and on a set that isn't field-ready, some of those point to a detail number that was renumbered, deleted, or never drawn. It's mechanical to check and easy to miss, because tracing every single reference to its destination across a full set is tedious enough that it's one of the first things skipped under deadline pressure, on a walkthrough or an estimating pass alike.

## Why this gap survives even on well-run bids

None of this happens because anyone is careless. A job walk is scheduled and run by the owner or GC's team for a specific purpose, and every bidder's estimating team is working against the same bid deadline everyone else is working against — there simply isn't a step in either process whose job is to read the full set end-to-end for internal consistency. [The same timing math applies to when a conflict gets caught, not just whether it does](/blog/pre-bid-drawing-review-vs-ifc-timing/): a contradiction found before bid gets fixed with an addendum, at no cost to anyone. The identical contradiction, caught after award, is now a change order, because a contractor has already priced and signed around the version of the drawings that had the gap in it.

That's the actual argument for adding a document-level review to the bid window, distinct from the walkthrough and distinct from any single bidder's own estimating pass: it's not duplicating either one, it's covering the one thing neither is scoped to do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Isn't the pre-bid job walk enough due diligence before award?

A job walk answers site-condition questions — access, existing utilities, laydown space — that matter for pricing and means and methods. It doesn't touch whether the structural and MEP sheets agree with each other, or whether the specs contradict a drawn detail, because those aren't questions a site visit can answer. They only surface from reading the full document set against itself.

### Don't the bidding contractors catch these conflicts through the RFI process?

Sometimes, but only incidentally. A contractor submits an RFI when something blocks their own pricing or scope, not as part of a systematic check of every discipline against every other discipline. A conflict that doesn't affect any one bidder's specific scope can sit in the set through the entire bid period without triggering an RFI from anyone.

### What's the difference between an estimator's takeoff and a pre-bid drawing review?

A takeoff extracts quantities and scope to build a price — it's working sheet by sheet toward a number, on a deadline shared with every other bidder. A drawing review reads the set for internal consistency: does this sheet agree with that one, do the specs match the drawings, do the callouts point to something that actually exists. Neither replaces the other; they're answering different questions.

### Does a pre-bid drawing review replace the walkthrough or the estimating process?

No — it runs alongside both. The walkthrough still covers site conditions, and every bidder still does their own estimate. A pre-bid drawing review adds the one pass that's structurally missing from either: a full read of the set for cross-discipline and spec-versus-drawing conflicts, done before any contractor has priced or signed around them.

### What happens to a conflict that's missed at bid and caught later?

It gets caught eventually — the physical world doesn't let a structural-versus-MEP clash or an unresolved spec conflict simply disappear. The cost is what changes. [The same conflict is far cheaper to fix as a pre-bid addendum than after a contractor has priced and been awarded the work](/blog/pre-bid-drawing-review-vs-ifc-timing/), because after award, fixing it means paying someone to change a scope they already signed a price against.
