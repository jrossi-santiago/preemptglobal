!lede The cost of design errors and omissions doesn't disappear on a design-build project — it just stops following the rule most owners and GCs learned on design-bid-build jobs. Under a traditional delivery structure, the Spearin doctrine draws a clean line: the owner warrants the design it hands to the contractor, so a contractor who builds to the plans isn't on the hook for what's wrong with them. Design-build puts design and construction under one contract, held by one party, and that single fact changes who's presumed to own a design defect before a single drawing is even reviewed.

## The baseline shift: from owner-warrants to design-builder-warrants

Under the Spearin doctrine — the implied warranty from the 1918 *United States v. Spearin* decision that plans and specifications an owner furnishes are adequate for their intended use — a design-bid-build contractor who builds according to the owner's design isn't liable for the consequences of a defect in that design. That's the default most construction risk allocation gets taught against.

Design-build changes the starting point. When the design-builder is the party developing and furnishing the design — not just building to someone else's — courts have generally held that it's the design-builder, not the owner, who impliedly warrants the design's adequacy and sufficiency. That's the core mechanism of a design-build contract as a risk-transfer tool: single-point responsibility means the same entity that made the design decision is also the one executing it, so the traditional owner-warrants-the-design logic doesn't have anywhere to attach. The exposure that Spearin would have assigned to the owner on a design-bid-build job instead sits with the design-builder.

That's the reason owners are often drawn to design-build in the first place — not just schedule compression, but a genuine shift of design risk off their books and onto the party best positioned to control it.

## The exception that swallows part of the rule: owner-furnished design

The shift isn't total, and this is where exposure actually gets missed. Design-build projects routinely start with owner-furnished design material — bridging documents, a schematic or design-development package from an owner's architect, performance criteria the design-builder has to design to rather than originate. When the owner supplies part of the design instead of a pure performance spec, that part doesn't automatically inherit the design-builder's warranty.

Courts evaluating disputes over owner-furnished bridging documents have generally continued to apply Spearin-style reasoning to the pieces the owner actually authored: if the owner's bridging documents are incomplete or inaccurate, the owner can still be exposed to an implied-warranty claim for that portion, even though the design-builder holds primary responsibility for finishing and coordinating the overall design. Who controlled a given design decision — not which single contract everything sits under — is what actually determines who owns the resulting exposure.

Government-procured design-build work follows a similar pattern: when the owner's solicitation package includes a substantial amount of pre-developed design rather than pure performance requirements, the design-builder assumes correspondingly less of the design-error risk, and has more grounds to recover costs tied to defects in what the owner provided. The more design control an owner retains before handing the project to a design-builder, the more of the Spearin exposure it keeps — regardless of the contract structure sitting on top of it.

## What the data shows: exposure concentrates, it doesn't vanish

The Federal Highway Administration's Design-Build Effectiveness Study, comparing design-build and design-bid-build delivery on completed projects, found that design-build jobs generated fewer change orders overall than comparable design-bid-build jobs — but the average cost per change order was higher on the design-build projects. Change orders ended up representing about the same share of total project cost across both delivery methods.

That's a materially different picture than "design-build reduces design-error cost exposure." It's closer to: design-build produces fewer, larger-dollar change order events, landing on roughly the same total cost as the rework a design-bid-build project generates through more, smaller ones. The delivery method redistributes when and how the exposure surfaces — concentrated in bigger single events instead of spread across smaller ones — without reliably shrinking the total.

For a design-builder, that pattern matters directly: fewer events sounds like less risk, but each one is a larger single exposure to absorb under a warranty the design-builder itself now holds, instead of a series of smaller change orders that would have been priced and passed through under a design-bid-build GC's more familiar process.

## The insurance gap that turns a warranty into a balance-sheet problem

The design-builder's exposure doesn't automatically transfer to its professional liability coverage, and that gap is where a design-error cost can outrun what a design-builder expects to pay. Professional errors-and-omissions insurance responds to a breach of the professional standard of care — negligence — not to a contractual warranty. A design-build contract that folds design services into the general scope of "Work," and then requires the design-builder to indemnify the owner broadly for anything arising from that Work, can create an obligation stricter than what the design-builder's own E&O policy is written to cover.

Concretely: if a design-build contract's warranty or indemnity language commits the design-builder to a standard beyond ordinary negligence — a guarantee of fitness or suitability, for instance, rather than reasonable professional skill and judgment — a design defect that would be covered under a negligence-based claim can fall outside what the E&O carrier will pay, leaving the difference as a direct cost to the design-builder. That's a separate risk from who bears the exposure in the first place; it's about whether the party bearing it can actually fund the fix once it's identified.

## Where this leaves the practical question: who's checking the design before it locks in

None of this changes the fact that a design conflict is cheapest to fix before it's built, regardless of who ends up paying for it after the fact. What design-build changes is which party has the clearest incentive to catch it early, and that incentive tracks the exposure split above:

- **Design-builder-originated design** — the design-builder holds the warranty and, per the FHWA data, is more likely to face a smaller number of larger-dollar change orders rather than many small ones. That argues for a document check before each package locks in, not after — similar to [what changes when design and construction overlap on a design-build schedule](/blog/catching-design-errors-before-construction-design-build-overlap/).
- **Owner-furnished bridging or criteria documents** — the owner retains Spearin-style exposure for what it actually authored, which means the owner has a direct incentive to have those documents checked for internal coordination and completeness before they're issued to design-build teams, not after a design-builder has already priced and contracted around them.
- **Either party's insurance exposure** — because E&O coverage tracks a negligence standard rather than a contractual warranty, the practical fix isn't better insurance language alone; it's catching the defect before it becomes a claim in the first place, the same logic covered in [what E&O insurance actually responds to on a design-error claim](/blog/eo-insurance-cost-of-design-errors-and-omissions/).

The allocation question — design-builder or owner, self-performed or GC-led, [as covered in the delivery-method comparison](/blog/cost-of-design-errors-and-omissions-self-performed-vs-gc-led/) — determines who writes the check when a design error turns into a cost. It doesn't determine whether that cost happens at all. That's still a function of whether anyone reviewed the cross-discipline set, owner-furnished and design-builder-developed portions alike, before it was locked into a single contract that makes untangling the error afterward far more expensive than catching it before.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does design-build eliminate the owner's exposure to design error costs?

No. Design-build shifts most of the design warranty to the design-builder for the design it develops, but an owner that furnishes bridging documents, schematic design, or performance criteria can still carry Spearin-style exposure for whatever it actually authored. The exposure follows who controlled the design decision, not just which contract the work sits under.

### Who pays for a design error on a design-build project?

It depends on who originated the specific design element at issue. For design the design-builder developed itself, the design-builder generally bears the cost under the implied warranty it holds. For design the owner furnished — bridging documents or criteria the design-builder had to design to — the owner can retain exposure if that furnished material was incomplete or inaccurate.

### Does design-build actually reduce the cost of design errors compared to design-bid-build?

Not reliably. FHWA's Design-Build Effectiveness Study found design-build projects had fewer change orders than comparable design-bid-build projects, but a higher average cost per change order, with change orders representing a roughly comparable share of total project cost across both methods. Design-build changes how the cost surfaces more than whether it happens.

### Does a design-builder's professional liability insurance cover every design error cost?

Not necessarily. Professional E&O coverage responds to a breach of the standard of care — negligence — not to a contractual warranty. If a design-build contract's indemnity or warranty language commits the design-builder to a stricter standard, a design-error cost can exceed what the E&O policy is written to pay, leaving the design-builder to fund the gap directly.

### How can a design-builder or owner reduce this exposure before it becomes a change order?

The only point where the exposure is still cheap to fix is before the design locks into the contract or gets built — a cross-discipline document review that checks both design-builder-developed and owner-furnished portions for coordination and completeness before issuance, rather than relying on warranty or indemnity language to sort out who pays after the fact.
