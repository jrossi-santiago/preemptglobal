!lede An owner with an internal QA/QC function has a reasonable question before bringing in a constructability review consultant: don't we already check for this? The honest answer is usually no — not because the in-house team is doing its job poorly, but because in-house QA/QC and a constructability review consultant are built to catch two different kinds of problems. Most owner-side QA/QC programs were never scoped to do what a dedicated, cross-discipline document read does, and most constructability reviews aren't scoped to do what in-house QA/QC does either.

That's not an argument for running one instead of the other. A capable in-house QA/QC function is one of the more valuable functions on a capital program, and skipping it would be a mistake. The real question isn't in-house QA/QC or a constructability review consultant. It's whether the in-house function, scoped the way it's scoped on most projects, already covers what a constructability review checks — and on the majority of multi-discipline builds, it doesn't, for reasons that have nothing to do with how good the internal team is.

## What in-house QA/QC actually checks

An owner's internal QA/QC function is typically built around process and compliance, not a document-by-document cross-discipline read. On a well-run program, it covers:

- **Deliverable tracking against the contract** — confirming design and construction deliverables show up on schedule and match what the owner's agreements require at each phase.
- **Spec and code conformance, procedurally** — verifying the design team's own QC sign-offs happened, submittals were reviewed and logged, and required inspections were performed and documented.
- **Process adherence** — making sure the quality management system defined for the program is actually being followed: who reviewed what, when, and against which standard.
- **RFI and submittal-log oversight** — tracking open items, response times, and whether questions raised during design or construction are getting closed out.

That's real, necessary work, and on a program without it, deliverables slip and nobody notices until a deadline is missed. What it isn't built to do is read the mechanical set against the structural set against the spec sections looking for a contradiction between three documents that each individually pass every check on the list above.

## What a constructability review consultant actually checks

A constructability review consultant runs a different exercise entirely: a full drawing and spec review across every discipline in the set, specifically hunting for places where one discipline's documents contradict another's. The deliverable is a written findings report — every conflict located to a specific sheet, ranked by severity, with a dollar exposure and schedule impact attached to each item, not just a description of the problem.

That distinction is the same one that shows up between an architect's internal QC and a coordination review, covered in more depth in [why passing QA/QC doesn't mean a set is coordinated](/blog/qa-qc-passed-doesnt-mean-set-coordinated/) — a mechanical sheet and a structural sheet can each be internally clean and still conflict with each other, because internal consistency and cross-discipline agreement are two different standards. The same gap exists on the owner's side of the table: an in-house QA/QC program can confirm every deliverable arrived, every submittal was logged, and every discipline's own QC sign-off happened, while the actual cross-discipline conflict sits untouched in the space between documents that no item on that checklist was ever built to catch.

## Where the two genuinely overlap

There's real overlap, and it's worth naming so the distinction doesn't get overstated. Both functions care about code compliance. Both will catch some spec violations. Both create a paper trail that's useful if a dispute comes up later. And a strong in-house QA/QC team with construction-document experience will occasionally flag a coordination issue by instinct, even though it isn't what the process is built to systematically find.

That overlap is exactly why the "don't we already check for this" question is a reasonable one to ask, and not a naive one. The overlap is also why it's easy for an owner to assume coverage exists where it doesn't — the two functions produce enough shared output (inspection logs, RFI tracking, code sign-offs) that it can look, from the outside, like the full set has been read against itself, when what's actually happened is that each discipline's documents have been checked against that discipline's own standard, run through a compliance process, and logged.

## When in-house QA/QC alone is enough

Not every project needs a separate constructability review consultant on top of a solid in-house QA/QC function. The cases where in-house coverage is genuinely sufficient tend to share a few traits:

- **A design team the owner has worked with repeatedly**, with a track record of low change-order rates and few cross-discipline surprises reaching the field.
- **A single-discipline or low-complexity scope**, where the number of interfaces between disciplines that could actually conflict is small.
- **An in-house team with the bandwidth and construction-document background to do a genuine cross-discipline read**, not just compliance tracking — some owner QA/QC functions are staffed and scoped broadly enough to do this themselves, which is exactly the coverage a constructability review consultant would otherwise provide.

Outside of those conditions, in-house QA/QC and a constructability review consultant are answering different questions, and running only one leaves the other unanswered.

## When a project needs both

Complexity is the trigger, not project size. A set with multiple trades in tight coordination — MEP routing sharing ceiling space with structure, a spec section that has to agree with three different disciplines' drawn details, a design team assembled from several firms for the first time — is exactly the condition where in-house QA/QC's process coverage and a constructability review's cross-discipline coverage stop being redundant and start being complementary. [What a constructability review consultant actually delivers, project by project](/blog/what-a-constructability-review-consultant-delivers-project-by-project/) covers how that cross-discipline read gets scoped differently depending on the asset class — but the underlying logic holds regardless of building type: the more disciplines whose documents have to agree with each other, the more a dedicated cross-discipline read earns its place next to the compliance tracking in-house QA/QC already provides.

Timing matters here too. Bringing a constructability review consultant in alongside in-house QA/QC works best inside the same window that governs any independent review — [there's a real window for when to hire a drawing review consultant](/blog/when-to-hire-a-drawing-review-consultant/), typically opening around 50% construction documents and staying open through 75–90% CDs, while a finding is still a redline instead of a change order. Running the two functions in parallel during that window, rather than treating a constructability review as a fallback after in-house QA/QC flags a problem, is what actually closes the gap before it reaches bid.

The two functions aren't competing for the same job. In-house QA/QC verifies the process happened and the deliverables are compliant. A constructability review consultant verifies the documents actually agree with each other across every discipline. A project with real coordination complexity needs an answer to both questions — and only one of the two functions, as typically scoped, is built to answer the second one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does in-house QA/QC replace the need for a constructability review consultant?

Usually not. In-house QA/QC is typically scoped around deliverable tracking, process adherence, and spec conformance — verifying that the right reviews happened and were logged. A constructability review consultant runs a dedicated cross-discipline read of the documents themselves, looking for contradictions between disciplines that a process-focused QA/QC check isn't built to catch.

### What's the actual difference between QA/QC and a constructability review?

QA/QC confirms that a process was followed correctly — deliverables arrived, submittals were reviewed, code sign-offs happened. A constructability review checks whether the documents produced by that process actually agree with each other across disciplines. A set can pass every QA/QC check and still contain a cross-discipline conflict, because the two checks are scoped to different questions.

### Can an owner's in-house team do a cross-discipline coordination check itself?

Some can, if the team is staffed with construction-document experience and has the bandwidth to read the full set against itself before bid rather than tracking compliance. Most in-house QA/QC functions aren't scoped or staffed for that specific exercise, which is why a dedicated constructability review consultant fills the gap on complex, multi-discipline sets.

### When is in-house QA/QC alone enough, without a separate constructability review?

It tends to be sufficient on lower-complexity scopes, with a design team that has a track record with the owner and few cross-discipline surprises historically, or when the in-house team is specifically staffed and scoped to do a genuine cross-discipline read rather than just process tracking.

### When should a constructability review consultant be brought in alongside in-house QA/QC, rather than after?

Ideally at the same point any independent review should start — around 50% construction documents through 75–90% CDs — so both functions are running in parallel while a finding is still a redline instead of a change order, rather than treating the review as a fallback once in-house QA/QC has already flagged something it wasn't scoped to fully diagnose.
