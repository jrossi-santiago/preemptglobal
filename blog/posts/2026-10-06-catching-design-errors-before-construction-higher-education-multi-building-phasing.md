!lede Catching design errors before construction is harder on a higher education capital program than on a standalone building, because the buildings are not independent. A university funds a residence hall, a lab building, and a utility upgrade as one program, and the sequence matters: classes have to keep running, occupants have to move somewhere, and one building often can't start until another is finished. A mistake in a single set can stall everything behind it.

## Why phasing raises the stakes

On a single building, a coordination error costs you an RFI, a revised sheet, or a change order on that project. On a phased campus program, the same error can move a handoff date. If the replacement building is late, the building scheduled for demolition stays occupied, and every contractor waiting on that site waits too.

That is the difference. The error is not bigger on the page. It sits on the critical path of other work, so its cost grows with the number of dependencies that run through it.

Three conditions create that exposure:

- **Dependent sequence.** Building B can't be demolished until its occupants are in Building A. Building C's site is Building B's footprint.
- **Staggered documents.** Sets reach construction documents at different times, often from different design teams. The first set issued fixes assumptions the later ones inherit.
- **Occupied surroundings.** Work happens next to live classrooms, labs, and residence halls, so access, noise, egress, and utility shutdowns are constraints on the drawings, not afterthoughts.

The general method is covered in the [checklist for catching design errors before construction](/blog/catching-design-errors-before-construction-checklist/). A multi-building program adds the questions below.

## Where multi-building sets fail

### Phasing sheets that disagree with the specifications

The phasing plan shows the sequence. The front-end specifications describe it again in words: milestones, work-hour limits, shutdown notice periods, owner-occupied areas. When the sheet says one thing and the spec says another, bidders price different jobs. Read the phasing drawings and the Division 01 requirements together, and check that every milestone named in one appears in the other with the same trigger.

### Swing space that nobody drew in full

Swing space is the temporary home for occupants during construction. It is often a building or floor that is itself being renovated. Check that the swing space has what its occupants need on the date they arrive: power, data, HVAC, egress, and accessibility. A swing space shown as "existing, no work" on one sheet and as a scope item on another is a common finding, and it is a schedule problem, not just a drawing problem.

### Shared utilities between buildings

Chilled water, hot water or steam, electrical service, and telecommunications are usually designed as campus systems and tapped by each building. Check that the load each building's mechanical and electrical sheets assume matches what the utility set delivers at the connection point. Then check the sequence: a building that needs chilled water for startup can't be commissioned if the loop extension is scheduled after it.

### Temporary conditions

Temporary power, temporary chilled water, and temporary fire access often appear only on a phasing sheet, if they appear at all. They also have to show up in the mechanical, electrical, and civil scopes with someone responsible for building and removing them. A temporary condition with no owner in the specs is a change order waiting for a bidder to notice it.

## Spec drift between buildings

Owners usually issue a master specification or design standard for the program. Over the months between one set and the next, individual teams edit sections for their building. A side-by-side read often finds the same section requiring different products, submittals, or testing from one building to the next. When the same trades bid more than one building, that discrepancy turns into pricing confusion and later into RFIs. The single-set version of this problem is described in [spec vs. drawing conflicts](/blog/spec-vs-drawing-conflicts/). On a program, add spec against spec.

## A practical order of operations

Teams that catch problems early tend to sequence the work the same way.

1. **Review the earliest set first.** Its assumptions travel into every later set, so errors found here save work downstream.
2. **Trace the critical path through the drawings.** List the handoffs between buildings (occupant moves, demolition triggers, utility tie-ins) and confirm each one is drawn, specified, and scheduled consistently.
3. **Read the shared-systems sheets against each building.** Source to point of use, for every connection.
4. **Carry findings forward.** A detail that failed in the first building is probably in the second. Log it and check before that set is issued.
5. **Re-check after addenda.** A bulletin that changes one building's scope can break a handoff that was fine at bid.

## Who should do the checking

The architects and engineers on each building know that building well. A third-party reviewer adds a reader who sees all the sets together and has no stake in any one of them. For the owner's side of this, see [owner's representative drawing review for multi-building higher education programs](/blog/owners-representative-drawing-review-higher-education-capital-programs-multiple-buildings/) and the [multi-phase program review](/blog/owners-representative-drawing-review-multi-phase-programs/). For one building that mixes labs and classrooms, the [higher education lab and classroom checklist](/blog/document-review-checklist-higher-education-lab-classroom-hybrid/) is a useful companion.

The goal isn't to second-guess anyone's design. It's to make every team's job easier by closing gaps between sets before bidders find them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does catching design errors before construction mean on a campus program?

It means finding coordination conflicts while they are still sheet revisions, before bidders price them. On a campus program that includes conflicts between buildings: phasing, swing space, and shared utilities, in addition to the usual conflicts between disciplines within one set.

### Why is phasing a common source of design errors?

Phasing information lives in several places: the phasing plan, the specifications, the schedule, and each discipline's scope. When those are edited at different times, they drift apart. Bidders then price different sequences.

### When should a multi-building program be reviewed?

Before each set is issued for bid, starting with the earliest. Review again after addenda or bulletins that change scope, since they can break a handoff that worked in the original set.

### Who is responsible for checking the line between buildings?

Usually no one is assigned it. Each design team covers its own building. An owner's representative or independent reviewer can read the sets together, which is where cross-building conflicts show up.
