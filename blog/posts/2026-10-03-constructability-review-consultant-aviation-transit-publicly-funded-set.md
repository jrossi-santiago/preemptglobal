!lede A constructability review consultant on an aviation or transit project is reviewing a set that has to be built around something still running: aircraft, trains, buses, passengers, and the security and safety rules that come with them. The drawings still have to coordinate across disciplines, but the set also carries funding-driven specifications, phasing sequences, and operational constraints that a typical commercial set doesn't. This is what changes, and where the review earns its keep.

## What "constructability review" means on a publicly funded transportation set

The core work is the same as anywhere: read the drawings and specifications as a builder would, and find the places where the documents disagree with each other or can't be built as drawn. If you want the baseline version of that work, [what a constructability review consultant actually delivers, project by project](/blog/what-a-constructability-review-consultant-delivers-project-by-project/) covers it.

Aviation and transit add a second layer. Many of these projects are funded in part by federal grant programs, and the funding comes with documents that govern how the work is specified and carried out. Two examples worth knowing:

- **Airports.** For projects funded under the Airport Improvement Program, FAA advisory circulars become part of the project. The FAA states that [AC 150/5370-2, Operational Safety on Airports During Construction](https://www.faa.gov/airports/resources/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.current/documentNumber/150_5370-2), is mandatory on AIP-funded construction, and that [AC 150/5370-10, Standard Specifications for Construction of Airports](https://www.faa.gov/airports/engineering/construction_standards/), is mandatory for federally funded airport projects. The first one drives the Construction Safety and Phasing Plan. The second brings a standardized specification set that has to line up with the project's own drawings.
- **Transit.** The Federal Transit Administration's [Project and Construction Management Guidelines](https://www.transit.dot.gov/sites/fta.dot.gov/files/docs/FTA_Project_and_Construction_Mgmt_Guidelines_2016.pdf) treat design reviews and constructability reviews as part of the design phase, and FTA's [Oversight Procedure 30](https://www.transit.dot.gov/sites/fta.dot.gov/files/2024-11/Oversight-Procedure-30-Value-Engineering-and-or-Constructability-Review.pdf) describes how FTA assesses a sponsor's value engineering and constructability review programs as a cost-control tool. Its [Readiness to Bid](https://www.transit.dot.gov/sites/fta.dot.gov/files/docs/OP53%20Readiness%20to%20Bid%20Construction%20-%20Sept%202015.pdf) procedure is the same idea applied at the point the set goes out for pricing.

## Where aviation and transit sets get harder to coordinate

### Phasing is part of the design, not an afterthought

On an occupied airfield or an operating rail line, the sequence of work determines what can be closed, when, and for how long. The drawings show phase lines, temporary conditions, detours, and tie-ins, and those pages have to agree with the permanent-work sheets and the specifications.

A constructability review follows a single scope item through the phases. Does the temporary power feed shown on the phasing plan exist on the electrical sheets? Does the demolition sheet remove something a later phase still needs? Does a lane or gate closure in the phasing narrative match the schedule constraints written into the specification? These are cross-document conflicts, and on an operating facility they can't be settled in the field without a shutdown someone didn't plan.

### Systems cross more discipline lines

Terminals, maintenance facilities, stations, and control buildings pack a lot of systems into the structure: baggage or fare handling, communications, security, life safety, fire protection, and specialty power. Each one is detailed by a different discipline and often a different specialty consultant. The risk sits at the handoffs, the same ones described in [MEP and structural interface points that fail most often](/blog/mep-structural-interface-points-fail-most-often/), with more systems stacked into the same ceiling space and the same slab penetrations.

### Specifications carry more weight

Federal and agency standard specifications are long and prescriptive. When they are attached to a project-specific set, the gaps show up as conflicts: a standard section references a material or test that the drawing notes call out differently, or a bid item description doesn't match the work shown. The patterns are the ones in [spec vs. drawing conflicts: where the two disagree](/blog/spec-vs-drawing-conflicts/), but the volume of attached boilerplate raises the chance something is stale.

### Existing conditions are rarely fully known

Airfield pavements, utilities, tunnels, platforms, and structures have long histories and incomplete records. A set that assumes an existing condition it can't confirm hands the risk to whoever builds it. The review flags the places where the drawings depend on an existing condition, so the owner can decide where to verify before bid and where to carry an allowance.

## Why the review has to land before the set is advertised

Publicly funded work is usually competitively bid, which narrows the correction path. Once the set is advertised, fixes become addenda, issued to every bidder inside a window set by the owner's procurement rules. That mechanic, and why it moves the review earlier, is covered in [pre-bid drawing review on public bid projects](/blog/pre-bid-drawing-review-public-bid-addenda-every-bidder/). On a project with a federal funding schedule or a fixed closure window for a runway or a rail line, a late addendum can also push the bid date, and a pushed bid date can cost a construction season.

The agency-side requirements an outside reviewer works under, such as confidentiality and conflict-of-interest rules, are laid out in [document review for public agencies](/blog/construction-document-review-services-public-agencies-procurement/).

## What to ask a constructability review consultant before hiring one for this work

Ask which of the project's governing documents they will read the set against, and whether they will review drawings and specifications together. Ask how they treat phasing sheets: as a separate deliverable or as part of the same cross-discipline pass. Ask whether findings are sheet-located and tied to a severity and a dollar and schedule impact, so the design team and the owner can act on each one. [Red flags in a weak proposal](/blog/constructability-review-consultant-red-flags-weak-proposal/) lists the signals to watch for when comparing responses.

The reviewer's job is to make the set clearer and easier to build for everyone who touches it: the design team that issued it, the agency that funds it, and the contractors who price it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a constructability review required on federally funded transit projects?

FTA guidance treats constructability review as part of design-phase project management, and its oversight procedures assess how a sponsor's program works. Whether a review is required on your project depends on the funding program and the agency's grant conditions, so confirm that with the funding agency.

### What does a constructability review check on an airport project?

It checks that the drawings, the specifications, and the phasing plans agree with each other and can be built in the sequence shown. On AIP-funded work, that includes how the project's documents line up with the mandatory FAA advisory circulars.

### When should a constructability review happen on a publicly bid set?

Before the set is advertised. At that point a correction is a revised sheet. After advertisement, it is an addendum issued to every bidder inside a regulated window.

### How is this different from the design team's own quality control?

Design-team QC checks each discipline's work against its own standards. A third-party constructability review reads the whole set as a builder would and looks for the places where disciplines or documents disagree. The two work together; see [how an independent review picks up where architect QC leaves off](/blog/architect-qc-vs-owner-review/).
