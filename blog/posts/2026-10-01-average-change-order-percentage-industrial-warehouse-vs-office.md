!lede Owners choosing between an industrial or warehouse build and an office building often ask for two numbers: the average change order percentage for each, side by side. A clean, sourced pair of figures doesn't exist. The peer-reviewed and industry data reports change orders across construction broadly, not by asset class. What can be said with confidence is where the two project types carry risk in different places, and why a set's coordination complexity predicts its change order exposure better than the word "warehouse" or "office" on the project name.

## What a search for the two numbers actually turns up

Look for a warehouse-versus-office change order comparison and the results are general: change orders average roughly 10% of contract value industry-wide, run **8% to 14%** in most survey-based ranges, and reach 25% or more on distressed projects. A records-based model published through the American Society for Engineering Education independently lands at **5% to 10%** added to contract price. Neither source separates industrial from office.

Cost-growth reporting does split by building type, but that is a different measurement. Warehouse construction cost inflation and office construction cost inflation describe how fast the price of building each type rises over time. They say nothing about how much a project's price grows after award through change orders, and mixing the two is the most common way a "warehouse vs. office change order rate" gets invented. For the broader gap in the data, see [what the data actually shows on average change order percentage by project type](/blog/average-change-order-percentage-by-project-type/).

## Why the two project types still carry different risk

The absence of a sourced average doesn't mean the two behave the same. They concentrate risk at different interfaces.

**Industrial and warehouse sets** tend to have a large, simple building envelope and a small number of very high-consequence interfaces. Slab design against racking and equipment loads, dock and door coordination, fire protection design against storage commodity and rack layout, and large electrical service to process or automation equipment all sit in few sheets but carry big dollar consequences when they disagree. Where the structure is tilt-up or pre-engineered, part of the set is authored by a specialty contractor or manufacturer rather than the design team, which adds a second authorship stream to reconcile. That is covered in [constructability review on industrial and warehouse projects](/blog/constructability-review-consultant-industrial-warehouse-tilt-up-pre-engineered/).

**Office sets** tend to run the other way: lower consequence per interface, but many more interfaces. Ceiling cavities shared by ductwork, sprinkler mains, lighting, and cable tray, multiple floors of repeated but not identical layouts, tenant-driven revisions, and an architectural finish package that touches every discipline. The exposure builds from volume of small conflicts rather than a handful of large ones, which is the pattern described in [where coordination-error costs actually come from](/blog/why-so-many-change-orders/).

## The warehouse that behaves like an office, and the reverse

Project labels hide more than they show. A distribution building with dense conveyor, sortation power, and a high-piled storage sprinkler design has more cross-discipline interaction than a simple speculative office shell. A multi-story office with heavy specialty spaces, such as a trading floor, a server room, or a lab-style area, picks up the coordination profile of a more technical building. A change order forecast built from the label will be wrong in both directions. One built from the number of disciplines competing for the same space, and how tight their tolerances are, will not.

## How to benchmark your own project instead

Rather than looking for a category average, price the set in front of you:

1. List the interfaces where three or more disciplines share a physical or load-bearing space.
2. Check whether the specifications and drawings agree at each one, including the sections most likely to disagree, per [spec vs. drawing conflicts](/blog/spec-vs-drawing-five-sections-most-likely-to-disagree/).
3. Identify any scope authored outside the design team (manufacturer frames, tilt-up panel details, specialty fire protection) and confirm it was designed against current loads and layouts.
4. Put a dollar figure and a schedule impact on each conflict before bid, not after.

Design errors and omissions alone have been estimated at roughly 3% to 5% of total construction budget, the slice of the change order total a cross-discipline review is designed to catch. That figure is not split by asset class either, but it applies to both a warehouse and an office, and it is the part owners can act on before bid. For the math of turning a set's conflicts into exposure, see [change order math: calculating real exposure before you bid](/blog/change-order-math-calculating-real-exposure/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are change orders higher on warehouse projects than office buildings?

There isn't a sourced study that answers that directly. Published change order figures are aggregated across construction at roughly 8% to 14% of contract value, and are not split by industrial versus office. Differences in risk come from the set's coordination complexity, not the label.

### What is the average change order percentage for industrial construction?

No confirmed figure is specific to industrial construction. The industry-wide ranges (about 10% on average, 5% to 10% in a records-based model) are the best-supported baselines. Any number presented as an industrial-only average without a cited source is likely the general range relabeled.

### Why do industrial and office projects have different change order risks?

Industrial sets concentrate risk in a few high-consequence interfaces such as slab loads, fire protection against storage layout, and electrical service, often with specialty-authored structure. Office sets accumulate risk through many shared ceiling cavities, repeated floors, and tenant revisions.

### Is construction cost inflation the same as change order percentage?

No. Cost inflation tracks how quickly the price of building a type of structure rises over time. Change order percentage tracks how much a specific contract grows after award. A building type can have high inflation and low change order growth, or the reverse.

### How can I estimate change order exposure on my own project?

Review the set itself: identify interfaces where several disciplines share space, check drawings against specifications at each, confirm any specialty-authored scope matches current loads, and price each conflict before bid.
