!lede Owners and capital planners weighing a higher education or lab building against a standard office often ask for two numbers: the average change order percentage for each, side by side. A clean, sourced pair of figures doesn't exist. The published data reports change orders across construction broadly, not by asset class. What can be said with confidence is that lab and campus buildings carry their risk in different places than an office does, and that the number of disciplines sharing tight space predicts exposure far better than the label on the project.

## What a search for the two numbers actually turns up

Look for a lab-versus-office change order comparison and the results are general: change orders average roughly 10% of contract value industry-wide, run **8% to 14%** in most survey-based ranges, and reach 25% or more on distressed projects. A records-based model published through the American Society for Engineering Education independently lands at **5% to 10%** added to contract price. Neither source separates higher education or laboratory work from office work.

Any figure presented as "the average change order rate for lab buildings" without a cited study is most likely the general range relabeled. For the broader gap in the data, see [what the data actually shows on average change order percentage by project type](/blog/average-change-order-percentage-by-project-type/), and for the parallel comparison on the other side of the spectrum, [industrial and warehouse versus office](/blog/average-change-order-percentage-industrial-warehouse-vs-office/).

## Why lab and campus buildings carry different risk

The absence of a sourced average doesn't mean the two types behave the same. They concentrate risk at different interfaces.

**Lab and higher education sets** pack a large amount of mechanical, electrical, and plumbing scope into each square foot. Fume hood exhaust and make-up air, pressurization relationships between rooms, specialty gases and lab waste, emergency power, and vibration-sensitive areas all compete with structure and ceiling space. Equipment is often owner-furnished or selected late, so room data sheets, utility connections, and structural supports have to be reconciled against equipment that may still be changing. Where the building houses cleanroom or containment space, classification drives additional detail, covered in [the document review checklist for cleanroom and containment lab buildings](/blog/document-review-checklist-cleanroom-containment-lab-buildings/).

**Standard office sets** tend to run the other way: lower consequence per interface, but many repeated ones. Ceiling cavities shared by ductwork, sprinkler mains, lighting, and cable tray, multiple floors of similar but not identical layouts, and tenant-driven revisions. Exposure builds from volume of small conflicts rather than a handful of expensive ones.

## The campus factors an office doesn't have

Higher education adds project conditions that sit outside the drawings but show up in them. Buildings are often delivered in phases against the academic calendar, with fixed occupancy dates that leave no room for field rework. Programs change as faculty, grant funding, and equipment needs firm up. Multiple buildings on one campus may share utility infrastructure, so a conflict in one set can affect another. These factors are examined in [coordinating across multiple buildings in a capital program](/blog/owners-representative-drawing-review-higher-education-capital-programs-multiple-buildings/) and in [the cost of design errors and omissions on multi-building programs](/blog/cost-of-design-errors-and-omissions-higher-education-multi-building-programs/).

## The office that behaves like a lab, and the reverse

Labels hide more than they show. A campus classroom building with straightforward systems has a coordination profile closer to an office. An office with a server room, a research suite, or a lab-style area picks up the profile of a technical building. Hybrid buildings are the hardest to predict by category, which is why [a checklist built for lab and classroom hybrids](/blog/document-review-checklist-higher-education-lab-classroom-hybrid/) exists. A change order forecast built from the label will be wrong in both directions. One built from the number of disciplines competing for the same space will not.

## How to benchmark your own project instead

Rather than looking for a category average, price the set in front of you:

1. List the interfaces where three or more disciplines share a physical space, starting with lab corridors, shafts, and ceiling cavities above specialty rooms.
2. Check whether specifications and drawings agree at each one, including equipment data sheets against room layouts.
3. Identify owner-furnished or late-selected equipment and confirm utilities, supports, and clearances match the current selection.
4. Put a dollar figure and a schedule impact on each conflict before bid, not after.

Design errors and omissions alone have been estimated at roughly 3% to 5% of total construction budget, the slice of the change order total a cross-discipline review is designed to catch. That figure is not split by asset class either, but it applies to a lab building and an office alike, and it is the part owners can act on before bid. For turning a set's conflicts into exposure, see [change order math: calculating real exposure before you bid](/blog/change-order-math-calculating-real-exposure/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are change orders higher on lab buildings than office buildings?

There isn't a sourced study that answers that directly. Published change order figures are aggregated across construction at roughly 8% to 14% of contract value and are not split by lab versus office. Differences in risk come from the set's coordination complexity, not the label.

### What is the average change order percentage for higher education construction?

No confirmed figure is specific to higher education construction. The industry-wide ranges (about 10% on average, 5% to 10% in a records-based model) are the best-supported baselines. A number presented as a higher-education-only average without a cited source is likely the general range relabeled.

### Why do lab projects have different change order risks than office projects?

Lab sets pack exhaust, make-up air, pressurization, specialty gases, and owner-furnished equipment into tight space, so more disciplines must agree at each interface. Office sets accumulate risk through many shared ceiling cavities, repeated floors, and tenant revisions.

### Do campus phasing and occupancy dates affect change order cost?

They affect how costly each conflict is. When a building must open for a fixed term, rework in the field has little schedule float to absorb it, so a conflict that would be a minor fix elsewhere can become an expensive one.

### How can I estimate change order exposure on my own project?

Review the set itself: identify interfaces where several disciplines share space, check drawings against specifications and equipment data at each, and price each conflict before bid.
