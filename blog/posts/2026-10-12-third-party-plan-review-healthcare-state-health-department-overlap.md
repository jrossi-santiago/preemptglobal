!lede Healthcare owners already live inside a layered review process: the local building department, the fire marshal, and in most states a health department or licensing agency that reviews facility plans before anyone can occupy the space. So the question comes up fast: if the state already reads the set, what does a third-party plan review add? The answer is that the state reviews for regulatory compliance, and a third-party plan review reviews for coordination. They touch the same sheets and ask different questions of them.

That difference matters more on healthcare than on almost any other building type. A healthcare set packs more trades into less ceiling, more equipment into each room, and more regulatory criteria into every wall than an office or warehouse does. The overlap with state review is real, and it is narrower than owners tend to assume.

## What state health department plan review actually checks

State practice varies, so confirm the rules for your jurisdiction before assuming anything. In most states, the health department or another licensing agency acts as an authority having jurisdiction for licensed healthcare facilities. The reference standard in many of them is the FGI Guidelines for Design and Construction, which [FGI tracks state by state on its adoption map](https://fgiguidelines.org/codes/adoption-map/). The Joint Commission points hospitals to either state rules or the current FGI edition for new, altered, or renovated space, and CMS leaves the choice of design standard to the organization.

What the state reviews against those standards is design criteria for a licensed use:

- Room sizes, clearances, and required adjacencies
- Infection control provisions, including handwashing station counts and locations
- Required ventilation rates and pressure relationships for specific room types
- Medical gas, essential electrical system, and emergency power requirements
- Finishes and patient-safety features that the guidelines specify

Some states change the process in ways that affect how much a state review tells you. Virginia, for example, adopted FGI as its criteria and accepts an architect's certification that the drawings comply, instead of reviewing every drawing. Minnesota publishes guidance on how FGI interacts with its other state and local fire, building, and zoning rules. In states like these, the state review confirms that someone answerable to the licensing agency stands behind code and guideline compliance. It does not tell you the plumbing, mechanical, and structural sheets agree with one another.

## Where the two reviews genuinely overlap

The overlap is not zero. There are four places where both reviews are reading the same content.

- **Room-level requirements that live in more than one place.** A pressure relationship in an isolation room or an air-change rate in an operating room appears in the mechanical drawings, the room data sheets, and the specifications. The state checks that the stated value meets the guideline. A third-party review checks that the three documents state the same value. When they disagree, the state may see only the one sheet it was handed.
- **Essential electrical and medical gas layouts.** The state reviews whether the required systems and branches exist. A third-party review looks at whether the routing of those systems conflicts with the structure, the ductwork, or the room above. We covered the equipment-level version of this in [spec vs. drawing conflicts on healthcare projects](/blog/spec-vs-drawing-conflicts-healthcare-equipment-room-data-sheets/).
- **Revisions triggered by state comments.** A state comment that changes a room's ventilation or adds a handwashing station creates a new drawing revision. That revision can collide with something already coordinated. A review that sees only the revised sheet never sees the collision.
- **Phased and renovation work in occupied buildings.** Interim life safety and infection control during construction draw attention from the state, and the same sequencing creates coordination exposure that only shows up when someone reads the phasing drawings against the demolition and new-work drawings together.

## Where they don't overlap: the clash-and-coordination gap

Most of what a third-party plan review is built to find has no regulatory angle, so it never appears in a state comment letter. A few examples of what that looks like on a healthcare set:

- A medical gas main routed through a corridor ceiling space where the mechanical set already shows a large duct and the structural set shows a transfer beam
- An imaging suite whose shielding, structural floor requirements, and equipment weight are each correct on separate sheets and inconsistent across them
- A pneumatic tube or conveyor path that clears the guideline-required clear dimensions and still hits a sprinkler main on another discipline's sheet
- A room data sheet that lists equipment the specifications do not include, or the reverse

Each of these meets the guideline when you read one discipline at a time. Each becomes an RFI, a field fix, or a change order when the trades build it. The same boundary applies to code review generally, and we described it in [where third-party plan review and AHJ permitting intersect](/blog/third-party-plan-review-ahj-permitting-intersection/). Healthcare adds a second regulatory layer on top of that, and the logic is identical: compliance review and coordination review are separate jobs.

## How to sequence the two reviews

Because state comments and third-party findings both force revisions, the order you run them in decides how many times a sheet gets redrawn. The lower-cost sequence is the same one we recommend for permitting. Run the third-party review against the set that is about to go to the state, so coordination fixes go in with the first submission instead of landing on top of a revision that responds to state comments. Then, when state comments do come back, have the revised sheets checked against the rest of the set before they are reissued. That revision check is the part owners most often skip, and it is where a fix for one agency turns into a new conflict with another trade.

Timing also depends on the delivery method. On a design-assist or progressive design-build healthcare project, the trades are contributing to the set while the state is reviewing it. We wrote about [bringing a third-party review into that kind of process](/blog/third-party-plan-review-design-assist-trade-partners-early/) separately.

## What to ask before you decide

A few questions will tell you how much your state review leaves open:

1. **Does the state review the drawings, or accept a certification?** If it accepts a certification, the state is relying on the design team's own check.
2. **Does the state review revisions, or only the initial submission?** Many owners assume every bulletin gets re-reviewed. Confirm it with the agency.
3. **Who reconciles room data sheets against mechanical, electrical, and specification content?** If the answer is "the design team, during QC," you have one set of eyes on a document set with at least three authors.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does state health department plan review replace a third-party plan review?

No. The state reviews a healthcare set against licensure rules and design criteria for the facility type. A third-party plan review looks for conflicts between disciplines and between drawings and specifications. A set can satisfy one review completely and still carry exposure the other was built to find.

### Is third-party plan review required on a healthcare project?

Generally no. State or local approval is typically required to build and operate a licensed facility. A third-party plan review is an owner's choice, made to protect budget and schedule rather than to obtain approval. Check your jurisdiction's current rules, since requirements vary by state.

### When should a third-party review happen relative to state submission?

Before the first submission, against the set headed to the state. That way coordination fixes ride along with the original submission and don't create a second round of revisions after state comments come back.

### Does the FGI Guidelines adoption mean the state has checked coordination?

No. FGI adoption sets the design criteria a licensed facility must meet. It says nothing about whether the mechanical, electrical, plumbing, structural, and architectural sheets agree with each other. Confirm the specifics for your state with the responsible agency.
