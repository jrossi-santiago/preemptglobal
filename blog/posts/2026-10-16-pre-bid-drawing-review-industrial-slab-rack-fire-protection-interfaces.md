!lede On an industrial or distribution project, three things have to agree before a single rack goes up: the slab that carries the load, the racking that sits on it, and the fire protection that protects what's stored in it. A pre-bid drawing review of an industrial set starts at those three interfaces, because they are drawn by different parties, released on different schedules, and priced by different subcontractors who may not read each other's sheets.

## Why These Three Interfaces Come First

A conventional office set spreads its coordination risk across many trades and many small penetrations. A warehouse or distribution set concentrates it. The building is a big box, the structure is comparatively simple, and almost all of the value — and almost all of the dollar exposure — sits in how the floor, the storage system, and the sprinklers relate to each other.

Each one is typically produced by a different author:

- **The slab** is designed by the structural engineer of record, often with flatness, joint layout, and thickness driven by the end user's equipment.
- **The racking** is frequently a tenant or owner-furnished system, engineered by the rack supplier from a storage layout that may not exist yet when the base building drawings are issued.
- **The fire protection** is often a delegated design, with the sprinkler contractor producing hydraulic calculations and shop drawings after award, against a storage commodity and height that the drawings may state only loosely.

When all three are described accurately and consistently in the bid set, bidders price the same building. When they aren't, bidders price different buildings, and the difference comes back after award as a change order.

## The Slab: What the Racks and Equipment Will Ask of It

The slab on grade in a distribution building is a structural element, not just a floor finish. A review should check whether the slab drawings were developed against the actual storage and equipment loads, or against a generic assumption that predates the tenant's layout.

**Point loads and rack baseplates.** Storage racks transfer heavy concentrated loads through small baseplates. The slab thickness, reinforcement, and subgrade preparation have to be designed for those loads at the aisle and column-line spacing the racking will actually use. If the racking layout changes after the slab is designed, the slab may be checked against loads in the wrong places.

**Flatness and levelness.** Narrow-aisle and automated material handling equipment depend on tight floor tolerances, usually specified using the FF/FL system defined in ASTM E1155. The review question is whether the specified tolerance matches the equipment actually planned for the building, and whether the specification and the drawings state the same requirement. A tolerance in the spec that disagrees with a note on the slab sheet leaves the concrete contractor guessing which one governs.

**Joint layout against rack layout.** Control and construction joints are laid out for concrete behavior. Rack baseplates and anchors are laid out for storage density. If a rack anchor lands on or near a joint, someone has to resolve it, and it is far cheaper to resolve on paper than after the slab is cut.

**Embedded and recessed items.** Dock levelers, trench drains, pits, conveyor supports, and floor-mounted equipment all need recesses or embeds in the slab pour. Those items appear on architectural, civil, structural, and equipment sheets, and a review cross-checks that they agree in location and size before the pour sequence is set.

## The Racking: The Interface Nobody Fully Owns

Racking is where industrial sets most often have a hole in them. The base building design team may draw the building for a storage layout that is only conceptual, while the rack supplier's engineering arrives later. A review should look for these specific gaps:

- **Whether the storage layout is in the drawing set at all**, or only referenced. If aisle widths, rack heights, and flue spaces are not shown, the sprinkler design and the slab design are both working from assumptions.
- **Who is responsible for rack anchorage and the slab's capacity to receive it.** If the structural drawings assume anchors will be provided by others and the rack supplier assumes the slab was designed for them, no one has actually checked the connection.
- **Whether the stated rack height agrees across disciplines.** Rack height drives the sprinkler strategy, the clear height the building needs, and the structural loads. The number should be the same on the architectural, structural, and fire protection sheets.
- **Whether the layout agrees with column and bay spacing.** Racks that were planned around one column grid and a building drawn on another produce lost positions or field relocation of columns' neighbors.

## Fire Protection: Where the Storage Assumption Becomes a Cost

Fire protection in a high-piled storage building is not a generic sprinkler layout. The system type and design criteria depend on what is stored, how high, and how it is arranged — and NFPA 13, the installation standard most jurisdictions adopt, ties sprinkler design to those storage characteristics. That is why a review treats fire protection as an interface with the racking rather than as a trade in isolation.

**Commodity and storage height versus the sprinkler design basis.** The drawings should state the commodity classification and maximum storage height the system is designed for. If the notes are silent, or if they disagree with the owner's program, the sprinkler contractor will price one system and the owner may need another.

**Ceiling-only (ESFR-type) protection versus in-rack protection.** Whether a building can be protected from the ceiling alone or needs additional protection within the racks changes cost significantly and affects the racking design. A review checks that the system type shown, the rack configuration shown, and the clearances shown are all compatible with each other.

**Clearance below the sprinkler heads.** Sprinklers need defined space below them and around storage. That space competes with ductwork, cable tray, lighting, conveyors, and unit heaters hung from the same structure at the same elevations. This is the same pattern covered in [why so many change orders come out of the Division 21–28 handoffs](/blog/why-so-many-change-orders-division-21-28-fire-protection-plumbing-hvac-electrical-handoffs/): each trade routes correctly on its own sheet, and the conflict only appears when the sheets are laid over each other.

**Structure supporting the piping.** Sprinkler mains and branch lines are heavy when filled. The roof structure, particularly on pre-engineered or tilt-up buildings where the frame is designed to a loading assumption, has to carry them. [Tilt-up and pre-engineered industrial sets](/blog/constructability-review-consultant-industrial-warehouse-tilt-up-pre-engineered/) add a second authorship stream that makes this check more important, not less.

**Water supply.** The available water supply for the design has to be reflected in the civil drawings, the fire pump and tank provisions if any, and the sprinkler design basis. A flow test result that is old or missing is a pricing risk to every bidder.

## A Practical Order of Operations for the Review

When a pre-bid review of an industrial set has limited time — and on a bid schedule it always does — the order matters. A workable sequence:

1. **Establish the storage basis.** Find where the set states rack height, commodity, aisle width, and equipment type. If it is not stated, that is the first finding, because everything downstream depends on it.
2. **Check the slab against that basis.** Confirm loads, tolerances, joints, and embeds correspond to what the storage basis implies.
3. **Check fire protection against the same basis.** Confirm the system type, design criteria, and water supply match.
4. **Overlay the overhead trades.** Check sprinkler clearances against ductwork, power, lighting, and material handling at their drawn elevations.
5. **Check the specs against the drawings.** Tolerances, system criteria, and responsibilities for delegated design should read the same in both places. [Pre-bid timing](/blog/pre-bid-drawing-review-vs-ifc-timing/) determines how much of this can still be resolved by addendum.

Findings from this sequence are most useful when each is located by sheet, ranked by severity, and tied to the cost and schedule consequence, so a bidder or an owner can act on it directly. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should a pre-bid drawing review check first on an industrial building?

The stated storage basis: rack height, commodity classification, aisle layout, and the equipment the building is meant to support. The slab design and the fire protection design both depend on it, so a gap there is the highest-leverage finding.

### Why does rack height matter to both the slab and the sprinklers?

Rack height affects the loads that reach the slab through baseplates, the clear height the building needs, and the sprinkler strategy required under the installation standard. If the number differs between disciplines, each one is designing for a different building.

### Who is responsible for designing the fire protection system on an industrial project?

It is commonly a delegated design: the sprinkler contractor prepares calculations and shop drawings after award. That makes it important that the contract documents state the design basis clearly, since the contractor can only design to what the documents say.

### Can a pre-bid review catch slab flatness and levelness conflicts?

Yes, to the extent the documents disagree. A review can compare the tolerance in the specifications against the slab sheets and against the equipment the building is planned for, and flag mismatches before the concrete is bid and placed.

### Is this kind of review worth doing on a smaller industrial project?

The interfaces exist on any industrial set where storage, floor, and fire protection must agree, regardless of project size. The review is scoped to the set in front of it.
