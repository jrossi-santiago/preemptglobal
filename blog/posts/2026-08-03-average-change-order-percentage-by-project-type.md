!lede Owners looking for a clean table of average change order percentage by project type — healthcare here, industrial there, multifamily below that — run into a gap almost immediately: the published data mostly isn't collected that way. Here's what the actual studies measure instead, the few category-specific figures that do exist, and what predicts a project's change order exposure better than its building-type label ever will.

## What the industry-wide numbers actually say

The figure that shows up most consistently across contractor and industry-association sources is a range, not a single number: change orders typically run **8% to 14% of total contract value**, with a commonly cited average around 10%. On distressed or poorly coordinated projects, that figure has been reported as high as 25% or more of the original contract value. These numbers come from aggregated industry survey data — reporting on change orders across construction broadly, not sorted by asset class.

A separate, more methodologically rigorous data point comes from a peer-reviewed study published through the American Society for Engineering Education, which built a statistical model of change-order cost impact using verifiable site records — owner daily reports, actual change orders, drawings, and specifications — rather than contractor survey responses. That model found change orders typically increased contract price by **5% to 10%**, with the model explaining roughly 80% of the variance in that increase. It's a useful check on the survey-based numbers above: independently, a records-based methodology lands in a similar range.

## Why "by project type" isn't how the data is actually collected

The honest answer to the search behind this topic is that a clean, apples-to-apples table — data center vs. healthcare vs. multifamily vs. industrial, each with its own confirmed average change order percentage — doesn't exist in the published literature in a form that would hold up to citing. The studies that produce the 8–14% and 5–10% figures above report at the level of "construction projects" as a category, not broken out by building type.

Where the data does get sliced, it's usually by project size or delivery method, not by asset class. Studies on residential and light-commercial work have found average change order volume roughly doubling as contract value moves from under $250,000 to the $250,000–$500,000 band, and separate industry data has shown small projects averaging under 2 change orders while large projects average more than 11. On the cost side, one commonly cited breakdown shows average percentage cost increase moving from roughly 3.2% on the smallest projects up toward 5% on projects in the $1–5 million range. Project size and contract structure are the axes the data actually tracks — building-type category isn't.

## The category-specific data points that do exist

There are a handful of exceptions worth naming directly, because they're real and relevant even though they don't add up to a full type-by-type table.

**Design errors and omissions carry their own figure.** Change orders attributable specifically to architectural and engineering errors and omissions have been estimated at roughly 3–5% of total construction budget — a subset of the broader change order total, isolated by cause rather than by building type. That figure matters more than most because it's the slice a cross-discipline document review is built to catch before it ever becomes a change order, as covered in more detail in [the real cost of design errors and omissions](/blog/eo-insurance-cost-of-design-errors-and-omissions/).

**Healthcare construction gets discussed differently than general commercial, even without a hard study behind it.** Healthcare-sector trade coverage has repeatedly framed change orders under roughly 5% as the mark of strong preconstruction planning on hospital work, contrasted against a general commercial range closer to 10–15%. That's industry commentary and benchmarking language, not a peer-reviewed study with a disclosed sample — worth knowing about, not worth treating as a hard number.

Beyond those two, the confirmed, sourced data runs out. Anything more granular — a specific percentage for warehouse and distribution, higher education, or data center work — isn't something we could verify against a real study, so it's left out here rather than estimated.

## What actually predicts change order rate, if not the category label

The pattern underneath the size- and structure-based data above points to a better predictor than "project type": the number of disciplines that have to agree with each other, and how tightly their tolerances interact. A project labeled "industrial" with a straightforward structural and electrical scope behaves differently than a project labeled "industrial" with dense process piping, high-capacity power distribution, and specialty mechanical systems stacked into the same ceiling cavity — even though both carry the same category label. The second one is where coordination conflicts concentrate, and that's a function of trade density and interface count, not the label on the project.

This is the same mechanism covered in more detail in [where coordination-error costs actually come from](/blog/why-so-many-change-orders/) — the projects that generate the most change order exposure are the ones where multiple disciplines share tight physical or systems coordination, regardless of what asset class they're filed under. It's also why the math in [change order math: calculating real exposure before you bid](/blog/change-order-math-calculating-real-exposure/) starts from a set's actual coordination complexity rather than a category-based rule of thumb.

## What this means for owners trying to benchmark their own project

If there isn't a reliable published number for "average change order percentage" on your specific asset class, benchmarking against one is a dead end. The alternative that actually holds up is benchmarking against your own set: a documented, sheet-located review of where your specific drawings and specs disagree with each other, priced out discipline by discipline, before bid rather than after. That number is specific to the project in front of you — not an industry average standing in for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the average change order percentage in construction?

Industry-wide figures typically put change orders at roughly 8% to 14% of total contract value, with an average often cited around 10%. A separate, records-based statistical model published through ASEE found a similar 5% to 10% range for the increase in contract price attributable to change orders.

### Does the change order percentage vary significantly by project type, like healthcare versus industrial?

There isn't a reliable, sourced study that breaks change order percentage out cleanly by building type. The published data reports at the level of construction projects generally, or splits by project size and delivery method — not by asset class. Healthcare construction is discussed in trade coverage as targeting a lower rate (under roughly 5%) than general commercial (10–15%), but that's industry commentary rather than a disclosed peer-reviewed figure.

### Does project size affect change order percentage?

Yes — this is one of the few axes the data does track consistently. Studies have found change order volume and cost impact both increasing with contract value, with small projects averaging far fewer change orders than large ones, and average cost impact rising from roughly 3% on the smallest projects toward 5% on projects in the multimillion-dollar range.

### What percentage of change orders come from design errors and omissions specifically?

Change orders attributable to architectural and engineering errors and omissions have been estimated at roughly 3% to 5% of total construction budget. That's the category a cross-discipline document review is specifically built to catch before it reaches the field.

### If there's no reliable average for my project type, how should I benchmark my own project's change order risk?

Benchmark against the set itself rather than an industry average. A documented, sheet-located review that prices out where your specific drawings and specs disagree, discipline by discipline, before bid gives a number that's actually about your project — not a category-wide figure standing in for it.
