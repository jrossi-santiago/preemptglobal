Higher Education Cost Risk Capital Programs

The Cost of Design Errors and Omissions on Higher Education Capital Projects: Why Multi-Building Programs Compound Exposure

On a single building, a design error is a line item. On a multi-building campus program, the same error can be repeated, delayed, and passed along. That is how exposure compounds.

JR
Joe Rossi
Founding Principal
October 7, 2026·7 min read

The cost of design errors and omissions on a higher education capital project is rarely the cost of one mistake. A university funds a residence hall, a lab building, and a utility upgrade as one program, often with shared standards, shared systems, and a fixed funding calendar. A mistake that costs one change order on a standalone building can show up again in the next set, hold up a handoff, and land on a schedule the owner can't move. Multi-building programs don't just add exposure. They compound it.

What "compounding" means here

Compounding comes from three mechanisms. Each one turns a single error into several costs.

  • Repetition. Design teams reuse details, schedules, and specification sections across buildings. If a detail is wrong in the first set, it tends to be wrong in the second and third.
  • Dependency. Buildings are sequenced. A late or reworked building can hold up demolition, occupant moves, and the start of the next site.
  • Shared infrastructure. Chilled water, hot water or steam, electrical service, and telecom are usually designed as campus systems. An error at the source can affect every building tied to it.

The general framework for estimating exposure is laid out in an owner's framework for estimating the cost of design errors and omissions before bid. This post adds what a campus program changes.

The direct costs, multiplied

Every error carries the usual direct costs: the RFI, the revised drawings, the change order pricing, and the rework in the field. On a program, each of these can occur more than once.

If the same coordination conflict sits in three buildings' sets and is found in the field on the first, the owner pays for rework once. If it is found after all three are under construction, the owner pays three times, and the trades are pricing the repeat as a known problem, not an unknown one.

That's why the order of discovery matters. Finding a conflict in the first set, before bid, costs a sheet revision. Finding it after three buildings are bid costs three change orders and three schedule hits. We don't put a number on it here, because the multiple depends on how much was reused. The point is that the multiple exists.

The indirect costs campus owners feel most

On a campus, the largest costs often aren't in the change order log at all.

Occupancy and swing space

Phased programs depend on occupants moving at specific dates. If a replacement building is late, the old building stays occupied, swing space stays leased or committed, and the next move slips. Those costs rarely get charged to the design error that caused them. They show up in operating budgets, not construction budgets.

Academic calendar deadlines

A residence hall that isn't ready by move-in, or a lab that isn't ready for the start of a term, has a hard deadline. A change order on an office building can slide a few weeks. A campus building with a semester start date usually can't. The cost of a delay is whatever the owner has to do to cover the gap.

Funding timelines

Many campus programs run on bond proceeds, state appropriations, or donor commitments with their own schedules and conditions. A design error that raises cost mid-program can force a scope trade-off in a later building. That is a cost of the original error, even though it appears on a different project line. For the public-funding side of this, see change order risk mitigation for municipal and K-12 bond projects.

Worth knowing

A design error's cost is usually booked where it was found, not where it started. On a program, the first building's mistake can be paid for out of the third building's budget.

Why the contract usually doesn't absorb it

Owners sometimes assume that errors and omissions insurance, or the designer's contract, covers the cost. In practice, coverage and responsibility are narrower than that. Professional liability policies typically respond to a claim of negligence, not to every coordination gap, and recovery comes after the fact, usually after the schedule has already been damaged. We covered what owners should ask about in E&O insurance and the real cost of design errors and omissions.

Under design-bid-build, the owner generally carries responsibility for design adequacy toward the contractor. Under design-build, the split is different, as described in who bears the exposure when design and construction share a contract. Either way, the practical result is the same: preventing the error costs less than allocating it.

Where multi-building exposure concentrates

Based on how campus sets are typically organized, these are the places to look first.

  1. The first set issued. Its details become the template for later sets. Review it hardest.
  2. Shared-systems sheets. Confirm that the loads assumed on each building's mechanical and electrical sheets match what the campus utility set delivers.
  3. Phasing sheets versus Division 01. Milestones, shutdown notices, and work-hour limits have to agree between drawing and text.
  4. Master specifications. Sections edited building by building drift apart. The same section can require different products or testing from one set to the next.
  5. Temporary conditions. Temporary power, temporary chilled water, and temporary fire access need a named trade responsible for building and removing them.

The phasing side is covered in detail in catching design errors before construction on higher education capital programs.

Putting a number on it

Owners get more out of an exposure estimate when it is built per finding rather than as a percentage of construction value. For each conflict, estimate the direct cost of correcting it in the field, the number of buildings it affects, and any schedule or occupancy dependency it touches. Then compare that to the cost of correcting it on paper before bid.

We don't recommend using a rule-of-thumb percentage here. Published figures vary widely by project type and delivery method, and a campus program's reuse and sequencing are specific to that program. Each of our review reports lists exposure item by item, with sheet locations, so the owner sees the arithmetic. If you want the broader picture of how change order rates differ, see average change order percentage by project type.

Reducing the exposure

The most effective step is to review the sets together, early, with someone whose job is the line between buildings. An independent reviewer reads every set against the others, so a conflict found in the first building gets checked in all the rest before they're issued. That makes the architect's and engineer's work easier, because the team is correcting a sheet instead of answering the same RFI three times. It also gives the owner a documented exposure figure before the money is committed.

Key takeaways

  • On a campus program, error cost compounds through repetition, sequencing, and shared systems, not just through the size of the individual mistake.
  • The largest costs are often indirect: swing space, academic deadlines, and funding conditions, and they rarely show up in the change order log.
  • Cost tends to land on a later building's budget than the one where the error began.
  • Review the earliest set hardest, and check every later set for the same finding before it's issued.
  • Estimate exposure per finding, with the number of buildings affected, rather than as a flat percentage.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the cost of design errors and omissions?

It is the total cost an owner bears because a drawing set is wrong or incomplete: change orders, rework, delay, and the knock-on effects on occupancy and funding. Direct costs show up in the change order log. Indirect costs often don't.

Why are design errors more expensive on multi-building campus programs?

Because the buildings share details, schedules, and systems. A single error can be repeated across several sets, hold up a sequenced handoff, or affect a shared utility. Each of those turns one problem into several.

Does errors and omissions insurance cover the cost of design errors?

Not reliably. Professional liability coverage generally responds to a claim of negligence, and recovery happens after the damage is done. It is not a substitute for finding the error before bid.

When should a campus program review its drawing sets?

Before each set is issued for bid, starting with the earliest, and again after addenda or bulletins that change scope. Early review is cheapest because later sets inherit what the first one fixes.
