Construction Document Review Services: What's Actually in Scope

"Document review" means a different scope at every firm that sells it. Here's exactly what's covered by default, what's an add-on, and what to confirm before signing.

JR
Joe Rossi
Founding Principal
August 6, 2026·9 min read

Ask three firms what their construction document review services cover and you'll get three different answers — one quotes a discipline-by-discipline audit, another quotes a spot-check of a few sheets, and a third quotes something closer to a second opinion on the architect's own QC. None of them are lying. "Document review" doesn't have one fixed definition across the industry, which means the scope question isn't optional — it's the first thing to pin down before a set changes hands.

The baseline: what's always in scope

At its core, a construction document review checks whether a set of drawings and specifications is internally consistent — whether what the structural sheets show agrees with what the MEP sheets show, whether the spec sections agree with the drawings, and whether the disciplines that have to physically share space in the field actually coordinate on paper before anyone breaks ground. That's the baseline, and it should hold regardless of which firm is doing the reviewing:

  • Full drawing and spec review, across all disciplines — architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and any specialty consultant sheets in the set, checked against each other rather than in isolation.
  • A written findings report — every conflict located to a specific sheet, ranked by severity, with the cost exposure and schedule impact spelled out per item rather than a general narrative.
  • Confidentiality handled before anything moves — drawings are proprietary, and a review firm that asks for the set before an NDA is signed is asking the wrong way around.
  • A defined turnaround — a review with no committed timeline isn't a service, it's a queue.

That baseline scope shouldn't shrink based on project size or asset class. A warehouse shell and a hospital tower both have disciplines that need to agree with each other on paper — the review that checks for that shouldn't have a minimum square footage or contract value attached to it.

What the deliverable actually looks like

Scope isn't just which sheets get checked — it's what shows up at the end. A findings report that's genuinely in scope for a construction document review should let an owner act on it without a follow-up call to decode it: each conflict tied to the sheet where it lives, a severity ranking so the reader knows what to fix first, and both a dollar exposure and a schedule impact attached to each line rather than a single lump estimate at the bottom. Anatomy of a findings report walks through what that document looks like line by line — it's worth comparing against whatever sample a firm shows before signing, since a report that's just a list of comments without severity or dollar figures attached is a narrower deliverable than what "document review services" implies.

Where scope commonly narrows without saying so

Two things quietly shrink the scope of a review without ever being stated as an exclusion:

Discipline coverage. A review that only reads architectural sheets, or only checks structural against architectural but skips MEP, is checking a fraction of the coordination that actually causes field conflicts. Most cross-discipline clashes — ductwork against a structural member, an electrical rough-in that ignores a plumbing chase — live exactly at the interfaces a partial review doesn't touch.

Specs versus drawings. A review scoped to drawings alone misses the conflicts that live in the gap between what the spec section requires and what the drawing shows — a real and common failure mode, not an edge case. Spec vs. drawing conflicts covers how often that particular gap produces a finding that a drawings-only review would never catch.

Worth knowing

Before signing, ask a review firm directly: "which disciplines are covered, and are specs included or just drawings?" A vague answer to either question is itself the answer — it means the scope narrows somewhere the proposal didn't spell out.

What's a paid add-on, not baseline scope

Some firms bundle the following into every engagement; others price them separately. Neither approach is wrong, but it needs to be clear which one is on the table before comparing quotes:

  • AHJ handling. Submitting permit, bulletin, or addendum packages directly to the authority having jurisdiction is a distinct scope of work from finding the conflicts in the first place — it requires ongoing coordination with the jurisdiction, not just a one-time read of the set.
  • Revision re-review. A set doesn't stop moving after the first pass — bulletins, addenda, and RFI-driven revisions keep changing it. Whether those revisions get re-checked against the rest of the set, or whether the review firm's job ends at the first report, is a real scope boundary, not a minor detail.
  • A dedicated point of contact. On a single-project engagement, this rarely matters. On a portfolio with several active sets, having one person who already has context on the whole program changes how fast a question gets answered.
  • A cumulative findings tracker. Running a log of what's resolved versus still open, across more than one review pass, is a different scope than a single report — it's a portfolio-level view, not a one-time deliverable.

None of these are default inclusions on a flat-rate, single-set engagement, and that's a reasonable place to draw the line — but a firm should say so up front rather than let an owner assume the broader scope until the invoice arrives.

How this differs from adjacent services

"Document review" gets used loosely enough that it's worth separating from services that sound similar but check different things. Architect QC and in-house QA/QC are real processes run by the design team itself — competent, but not independent, and not scoped specifically to catch cross-discipline conflicts the way a third-party review is. BIM clash detection checks the model for geometric collisions, which is a narrower and different question than whether the drawings and specs agree with each other on paper. And a constructability review, run by the GC's own team, is scoped to buildability and sequencing rather than an owner-side second opinion. None of these replace an independent construction document review — they answer adjacent questions, not the same one — but conflating them is exactly how an owner ends up with a coverage gap they didn't know existed.

Confidentiality is part of the scope, not a footnote

What happens to the drawings themselves is as much a scope question as which disciplines get checked. An NDA should be in place before any set moves, and the reviewing firm should be able to say plainly what happens to the documents once the review is finished — the NDA question covers what to actually ask before handing over a set, since "we sign NDAs" isn't a complete answer without knowing what happens to the drawings after the review closes.

Key takeaways

  • Baseline scope for construction document review services should always include full drawing and spec review across every discipline, a severity-ranked written findings report, an NDA before drawings move, and a defined turnaround.
  • Discipline coverage and specs-versus-drawings-only are the two places scope most often narrows without being stated as an exclusion — ask directly.
  • AHJ submission handling, revision re-review, a dedicated point of contact, and a cumulative findings tracker are reasonable add-ons, not baseline inclusions — the question is whether a firm says so up front.
  • Architect QC, BIM clash detection, and GC-run constructability review all check different, narrower questions than an independent, owner-side document review — none of them substitute for it.
  • Confidentiality terms are a scope item, not paperwork — know what happens to the drawings both before and after the review.

The fastest way to compare two proposals for construction document review services isn't the price line — it's whether both quotes are actually describing the same scope. A cheaper quote that only reads drawings, skips two disciplines, and stops at the first findings report isn't a better deal than a fuller scope at a higher price; it's a different, narrower service wearing the same name.

Frequently Asked Questions

What disciplines should a construction document review cover?

All disciplines present in the set — architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing, plus any specialty consultant sheets — checked against each other, not read in isolation. A review that only checks one or two disciplines is missing most of the interfaces where cross-discipline conflicts actually occur.

Does construction document review include the specs, or just the drawings?

It should include both. Specs and drawings can contradict each other even when each is internally consistent on its own, and that gap is a real, recurring source of findings — a drawings-only review misses it entirely.

Is a live review meeting normally included, or is that extra?

That varies by firm, but it's reasonable to expect it included at no additional charge as an optional step, not a paid add-on — the client should be able to take it or skip it without it changing the price of the underlying review.

What's typically NOT included in a standard document review, and priced separately?

AHJ permit/bulletin/addendum submission handling, re-review of later revisions (bulletins, addenda, RFI-driven changes), a dedicated single point of contact, and a cumulative findings tracker across multiple passes are common add-ons rather than baseline inclusions on a flat-rate, single-set engagement.

How is document review scope different from BIM clash detection?

BIM clash detection checks a 3D model for geometric collisions between elements. A construction document review checks whether the drawings and specs themselves — as documents, not as a model — agree with each other across disciplines. They catch overlapping but different problems, and one doesn't substitute for the other.
