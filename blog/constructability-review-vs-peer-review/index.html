Constructability Review vs. Peer Review

The two terms get used almost interchangeably on a project team, but they check for different things, are run by different people, and catching one doesn't mean the other happened.

JR
Joe Rossi
Founding Principal
August 1, 2026·9 min read

Constructability review and peer review get used almost interchangeably on a lot of project teams, and that's a problem, because they're not the same check. A peer review confirms the design is right. A constructability review confirms the design can actually be built the way it's drawn. A set can pass one and still fail the other — and an owner who thinks a peer review covers constructability, or vice versa, has a gap in the document set they don't know is there.

The confusion is understandable. Both happen before construction starts. Both involve an outside set of eyes on the drawings. Both are meant to catch problems before they get expensive. But they're looking for different failure modes, run by people with different backgrounds, at different points in the process — and knowing which one a project actually had is the first step to knowing what's still missing.

What a Peer Review Actually Checks

A peer review is an independent review of the plans and specs, typically performed by another design firm with the same or a related discipline background as the original designer. It checks the design against the standards a designer would use to judge another designer's work: local building code compliance, adherence to standard design practice, internal consistency between sheets, and whether design criteria were correctly applied.

The person running a peer review is trained to answer questions like: Is this structural calculation correct? Does this design meet code? Did the mechanical engineer size this system properly? Do the general notes agree with the details? Those are design-quality questions, and a peer reviewer with design credentials is the right person to answer them.

What a peer review is not built to answer is whether the design, correct as drawn, can actually be built in the field without a crew hitting a physical conflict that wasn't visible from a desk. That's a different question, asked from a different vantage point.

What a Constructability Review Actually Checks

A constructability review brings someone who has actually built from drawings — not just designed them — into the process before the set goes to bid. The Construction Industry Institute (CII) defines constructability as the optimum integration of construction knowledge and experience into project planning, design, procurement, and field operations to achieve overall project objectives. In practice, that means a reviewer with field or document-production experience reads the set specifically looking for places where a component or assembly, as drawn, can't actually be installed the way the drawing shows — or where two trades' work will physically collide once it's built.

Worth knowing

A constructability review is explicitly not a peer review, a QC pass, or value engineering. It doesn't re-check whether the design meets code or whether the engineering is correct — it assumes that's already been handled — and it isn't an exercise in finding cheaper alternatives. Its one job is buildability: can this be constructed as drawn, by the trades who will actually build it, without a field conflict.

That distinction matters because it changes who's qualified to run the check. A peer reviewer is judged on design credentials. A constructability reviewer is judged on whether they've actually produced or built from documents like the ones in front of them — someone who has drawn a duct routing, or coordinated a structural grid against MEP risers, and knows from experience where those two things tend to collide on paper before they collide in the field.

Where the Two Checks Overlap — and Where They Don't

Both reviews can catch a version of the same coordination problem, which is part of why they get conflated. A peer reviewer checking internal consistency might notice that a general note contradicts a detail. A constructability reviewer might notice the same contradiction, but framed as "this can't be built as drawn" rather than "this is internally inconsistent." The finding can look similar on paper. The reasoning behind it, and the scope of what each reviewer is actually checking, is not.

Where they diverge is the cross-discipline, field-sequencing layer. A peer review is usually scoped to one discipline reviewing work in that same discipline, or an adjacent one — a structural peer review checks structural work, not whether a structural beam collides with a mechanical duct three sheets away. A constructability review is built specifically to look across every discipline at once, from the perspective of the crew that has to build all of it in the same physical space, which is exactly the gap a discipline-by-discipline QA/QC pass is structurally unable to catch.

CII research cited a payback ratio of greater than 10:1 on investments in constructability, and found that projects with intensive constructability implementation carried more than a 6% average cost performance advantage over projects where it was applied lightly or not at all. That's a specific, buildability-side benefit — not a substitute for the design-accuracy work a peer review does, but evidence that the two checks are protecting against genuinely different kinds of loss.

Why a Set Can Pass One and Still Fail the Other

Because the two reviews check different things, a set that's passed a rigorous peer review can still have serious constructability problems sitting in it, and a set that's had a constructability pass can still contain a code violation or a calculation error a peer reviewer would have caught. Neither review is a substitute for the other. An owner who commissioned a peer review and assumes constructability was covered — or the reverse — has a document set with a real, uncovered gap, and won't find out about it until a subcontractor hits it in the field, which is a far more expensive place to discover a coordination conflict that was sitting in the set the whole time.

That's also why the two checks tend to happen at different points and are commissioned by different people. Peer reviews are frequently required by code, by a lender, or by an owner's internal risk process, and tend to run discipline by discipline as the design develops. Constructability reviews are more often brought in once a set is substantially complete and ready to move toward bid — late enough that the design is stable, early enough that a conflict found is still just a redline, not a change order already priced into a subcontractor's bid.

Key takeaways

  • A peer review checks design accuracy, code compliance, and internal consistency — run by another design firm with related credentials.
  • A constructability review checks whether the design, as drawn, can actually be built in the field — run by someone with field or document-production experience, not design credentials alone.
  • CII research found investments in constructability paid back at greater than 10:1, with a 6%+ average cost performance advantage on projects where it was implemented intensively.
  • A constructability review is explicitly not a QC pass, a peer review, or value engineering — it assumes design correctness and checks buildability instead.
  • A set can pass one review and still fail the other; assuming one covers the other leaves a real, uncovered gap in the document set.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a constructability review the same as a peer review?

No. A peer review checks whether the design is correct — code compliance, calculation accuracy, and internal consistency — and is typically performed by another firm in the same or a related discipline. A constructability review checks whether the design, correct or not, can actually be built as drawn without a field conflict, and is performed by someone with field or document-production experience rather than design credentials alone.

Can a design pass peer review and still have constructability problems?

Yes, regularly. Peer review is usually scoped to one discipline checking work within that same discipline or an adjacent one, so it can miss a conflict that only exists between two disciplines' sheets — a duct routed through a structural beam, for example — because each sheet, read on its own, is internally correct. That cross-discipline gap is specifically what a constructability review is built to catch.

Who should perform a constructability review?

Someone with genuine field or document-production experience — ideally, reviewers who have drawn or built from sets like the one in front of them, not just designed to code. That background is what lets a reviewer visualize how the components on paper actually go together in physical space, rather than just checking that each sheet is internally correct.

When should a constructability review happen on a project?

Most effectively once the design is substantially complete but before the set goes out to bid. Early enough that a conflict found is still just a redline nobody has priced around yet; late enough that the design is stable and won't be substantially revised out from under the review.
