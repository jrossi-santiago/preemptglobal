RFI Reduction on Design-Assist Projects: Why Early Trade Input Doesn't Eliminate the Review Step

Design-assist is pitched as an RFI reduction strategy on its own — get the trades in the room early, and the questions never get asked. Here's why that's only half the story.

JR
Joe Rossi
Founding Principal
September 28, 2026·11 min read

RFI reduction is one of the standing arguments for design-assist delivery: bring a mechanical, electrical, curtain wall, or structural steel trade contractor into the design process early, let their constructability knowledge shape the drawings before they're issued, and the RFIs that a design-bid-build sequence would generate after bid never get filed in the first place. That logic is sound as far as it goes. It's also incomplete, because it treats "the trade partner gave input" and "the issued set reflects that input correctly against every other discipline" as the same event. They aren't, and the gap between them is exactly where RFIs on a design-assist project keep coming from — just later, and often from a different source, than the ones design-assist was supposed to prevent.

Why design-assist looks like an RFI reduction win by default

The core RFI reduction case for design-assist is real. In design-bid-build, the mechanical and electrical trade contractors who actually install the systems see the drawings for the first time when they're pricing the job — after every design decision is locked in. Anything in those drawings that isn't buildable as drawn becomes an RFI during construction, because that's the first point the installing trade has had a chance to look. Design-assist moves that knowledge earlier: the same trade contractor sits with the design team during design development, weighs in on routing, sequencing, and access before the drawings are finished, and a category of "this can't physically go here" RFIs never gets generated, because the drawing was never issued that way to begin with.

That's a genuine structural advantage over design-bid-build, and it's the reason design-assist keeps getting adopted on mechanically dense, tightly sequenced projects. The problem is what it implies by omission: that once a trade partner has signed off on an area during design-assist coordination, that area is settled for the rest of the project. It isn't, and treating it as settled is what lets a different category of RFI back into the set.

Worth knowing

Design-assist reduces the RFIs that come from a trade contractor seeing buildability problems for the first time after bid. It doesn't reduce the RFIs that come from a trade partner's early input going out of sync with a design that kept changing after that input was given — because that category of RFI has nothing to do with when the trade contractor joined the project.

The RFI reduction math design-assist doesn't finish

A design-assist mechanical subcontractor advises a routing path around a structural element in a coordination meeting during design development. That advice gets captured, the mechanical sheet gets updated to reflect it, and everyone in the room treats that corridor as resolved. Weeks later, the structural engineer relocates a beam or adds a shear wall for a reason that has nothing to do with that conversation — a lateral system revision, an owner-driven layout change, a value-engineering pass. The mechanical sheet still shows the routing the trade partner advised. The structural sheet reflects a decision made after that advice was given. Neither sheet is wrong on its own, and neither discipline necessarily knows the other one moved, because the routing was already treated as a closed item once the design-assist trade signed off.

That conflict doesn't disappear because a trade contractor gave good early input — it just moves downstream, and when it surfaces during construction, it generates exactly the kind of formal question design-assist was supposed to prevent. The trade contractor built the field crew's expectation that this area was already coordinated, so the RFI that eventually gets filed often carries more schedule pressure than a routine one, because nobody was watching that corridor for new conflicts once it had been marked resolved.

Where RFIs still originate on a design-assist project

None of these gaps require anyone to have done a bad job. A mechanical trade partner giving accurate advice, a structural engineer making a legitimate later revision, and a coordination model that resolves a clash the sheet-update process hasn't fully caught up to are all normal, individually reasonable events. The RFI shows up because nothing in the design-assist process is specifically responsible for re-checking a previously resolved area against everything that's changed since it was resolved — the trade partner isn't tracking the rest of the set, the structural engineer isn't tracking whether their revision touches a corridor mechanical already signed off on, and the architect coordinating both relationships is managing two separate conversations, not auditing whether they still agree with each other.

Design-assist projects also frequently run more than one early trade partner at a time — mechanical and electrical both engaged before drawings are final is common, curtain wall or structural steel design-assist running in parallel on larger jobs. Each of those relationships gets coordinated with the design team individually. What doesn't happen automatically is a check of the design-assist trades' inputs against each other, and an RFI that traces back to two early trade partners' inputs conflicting is one of the harder ones to diagnose quickly in the field, because both disciplines can point to a sign-off from a trade contractor who genuinely knew what they were doing.

This is the same gap covered from the third-party-plan-review side in what changes about a review once trade partners join early: early trade involvement is a real improvement in how buildable a design ends up being, but it doesn't create anyone whose job is checking that the input actually stayed correct against a set that kept moving. And it's a variation on the same problem covered in why the standard RFI reduction playbook doesn't fully apply to fast-tracked projects: design-assist, like fast-tracking, removes the single finished-set moment that most RFI reduction advice assumes exists, and replaces it with a rolling set of partial commitments that each need to be re-checked as the rest of the drawings continue to change around them.

What actually reduces RFIs on a design-assist job

Getting the RFI reduction benefit design-assist promises, without absorbing the RFI category it quietly reintroduces, means treating the areas trade partners already touched as a specific thing to re-check, not a category that's earned a pass:

  • Track design-assist input against the current state of the set, not the state it was given against. A routing decision coordinated against a structural drawing from a specific date needs to be re-verified if that structural drawing has moved since.
  • Check design-assist trades' inputs against each other, not only against the base design. Mechanical and electrical input each individually agreeing with the architect's drawings doesn't confirm they agree with one another.
  • Confirm the issued sheet, not just the coordination model, reflects the resolved clash. A clash closed out in a 3D coordination session should be verified on the 2D construction documents that actually get built and permitted from.
  • Don't let a trade partner's sign-off end scrutiny of that area. It's a reason to check what's changed there since, not a reason to stop looking.

That's a review question, not a design-assist process question — which is the point. Design-assist gets good information into the design earlier. It doesn't put anyone in the role of confirming that information is still accurate by the time the set is issued, because tracking every discipline's changes against every other discipline's prior commitments across the life of the design was never part of what the trade partner, the architect, or any single design-assist relationship was set up to do.

Key takeaways

  • Design-assist genuinely reduces one category of RFI: buildability problems a trade contractor would otherwise catch for the first time after bid.
  • It doesn't reduce, and can quietly reintroduce, a different category: conflicts created when adjacent disciplines revise a "resolved" area after the design-assist trade already signed off.
  • Multiple design-assist trades working in parallel each coordinate with the design team, but their inputs aren't automatically checked against each other.
  • A clash resolved in a shared coordination model isn't guaranteed to carry through to the issued construction documents without a specific check.
  • Treating a design-assist trade's sign-off as permanent closure is what lets these RFIs resurface late, with more schedule pressure than a routine one.

Design-assist is a real RFI reduction strategy for the category of problem it's built to catch, and none of this argues against using it. It's an argument for not stopping there — an early trade conversation is one input into a coordinated set, and confirming the set stayed coordinated after that conversation is a separate, ongoing check that design-assist doesn't perform on its own.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does design-assist reduce RFIs on its own, without a separate review process?

Partially. Design-assist reduces the specific category of RFI that comes from a trade contractor seeing buildability problems for the first time after bid, because that knowledge is already in the design. It doesn't reduce RFIs caused by adjacent disciplines revising a previously coordinated area afterward, or by two design-assist trades' inputs conflicting with each other — those require a separate check, not just early trade involvement.

Why would an RFI still come up in an area a design-assist trade partner already reviewed?

Because design doesn't stop moving after that trade partner's input is captured. A routing decision coordinated against one version of an adjacent discipline's drawings can be contradicted by a later, unrelated revision to that same drawing — a relocated beam, an added shear wall — that nobody re-checked the earlier sign-off against.

If mechanical and electrical are both on a design-assist contract, are their systems automatically coordinated with each other?

Not automatically. Each trade partner typically coordinates their input with the design team directly, which doesn't guarantee their inputs were also checked against each other. Two design-assist trades can each be well-coordinated with the base building design and still conflict with one another.

Does a clash resolved in a coordination model mean it won't generate an RFI later?

Not by itself. Coordination often happens in a shared 3D model, and a clash resolved there doesn't automatically mean the fix carried through to the issued 2D construction documents. That gap is worth confirming directly rather than assuming the model and the sheet stayed in sync.

When should a coordination review happen on a design-assist project, if not just once before bid?

As design-assist input gets locked into issued drawings, rather than waiting for one finished-set moment — design-assist projects don't necessarily have a single traditional bid-set point any more than a fast-tracked project does. The review should track where early trade input has been captured on sheets and re-check it against whatever else in the set has changed since.
