Constructability Review Consultant for Multifamily Podium Projects: What to Check Where Wood Frame Meets Concrete

On a podium building the concrete deck is a handoff between two trades, two tolerances, and two sets of drawings. The conflicts that cost money sit in that handoff, and a constructability review is where they get read together.

JR
Joe Rossi
Founding Principal
October 17, 2026·8 min read

A constructability review consultant brought onto a multifamily podium project is, in practice, hired to read one place on the set: the podium deck, where a concrete or steel base hands off to wood framing above. That handoff is where the concrete contractor, the framer, the MEP trades, the waterproofer, and the fire protection contractor all need information from each other, and where the drawings most often leave each of them assuming someone else has it covered.

What the consultant is actually asked to review

Anyone searching for a constructability review consultant for a podium deal is usually looking for something narrower than a general drawing check. The questions are practical. Can the concrete be placed to the tolerance the framers need? Do the anchors, hold-downs, and sleeves land where the wood-frame drawings say they will? Does the deck stay dry and drain? Will the schedule allow each trade to follow the last?

Those are constructability questions, not code-compliance questions. The fire-rated horizontal assembly and the structural transfer are covered in our earlier post on catching design errors before construction on a podium building. This post covers the other half: the physical build sequence at the interface, and the places the drawings stop short of it.

Check 1: Tolerances that don't match

Concrete and wood framing are built to different tolerances, and the drawings usually specify each in its own section without reconciling them.

A cast-in-place deck is placed to a concrete tolerance. The wood-frame structure that lands on it needs plates, walls, and shafts to line up with the grid above. When the as-built deck is out of position by more than the framing can absorb, the framer shims, offsets, or asks for a fix, and none of those are free.

What the review looks for:

  • Whether the specified deck flatness and levelness suit the wall framing and finishes that sit on it, and whether the structural general notes and the specification state the same tolerance.
  • Whether wall locations are dimensioned from a controlling grid that both the concrete and framing drawings share, or from two separate references.
  • Whether anchor bolt and embed placement tolerances are stated for the concrete contractor, rather than left to the wood-frame sheets the concrete crew may never receive.

Check 2: Anchorage and the shear load path through the deck

Wood-frame shear walls resist lateral load, and that load has to reach the podium below. The connection is made with hold-downs, anchor bolts, and sill anchorage set in or through the concrete. Those items are structurally critical and are set during the concrete pour, which means they have to be known before the deck is placed.

The coordination risk is timing and ownership. The wood-frame lateral system is often developed by a different engineer, or as a deferred submittal, than the podium design. The podium drawings need to show the anchorage locations, loads, and embedment the upper structure will demand, and the upper drawings need to match. If the anchor layout is still being finalized when the deck is released for pour, the field answer is drilled-in or epoxy anchors, reinforcement conflicts, or a redesign of what was already placed.

A review overlays the wood-frame shear wall layout on the deck reinforcement and post-tensioning layout, if present, and checks that every hold-down and anchor has a home that doesn't collide with steel or tendons.

Worth knowing

Anything that must be cast into the deck has to be fully defined before the pour. Items defined later by a deferred submittal become field-drilled work, which is where deck reinforcement and post-tensioning conflicts start.

Check 3: Deferred submittals that the deck depends on

Podium sets frequently route key components through delegated design: engineered wood trusses, the fire sprinkler system, sometimes the lateral system and the podium-level waterproofing assembly. Each is reasonable on its own. The issue is what each one needs from the deck, and when.

A constructability review traces each deferred item to the decision it forces on the podium:

  • Sprinkler mains and risers crossing the deck need sleeves and penetrations placed during the pour, but the sprinkler design may not exist until after the deck drawings are issued.
  • Truss and floor system bearing conditions affect the deck edge, the slab step, and the wall layouts below.
  • Mechanical, plumbing, and electrical risers passing through the deck need locations frozen early enough for the concrete contractor to sleeve them.

If the drawings say "by others" without saying who and by when, the review flags it as an open scope item for the bid, because every bidder will fill that gap with a different assumption.

Check 4: The deck as a waterproofing and drainage surface

On many podium projects the deck is not just structure. It carries courtyards, amenity space, landscaping, or pavers over the occupied or parked space below. That makes it a roof-type assembly with the same failure mode as any roof: water that finds a path.

The constructability questions are about continuity and sequence. Does the waterproofing membrane detail at the wall base, the drain, the expansion joint, and every penetration actually close, or does each trade's sheet end where its own scope ends? Are slopes to drains built into the deck structure, or left for a topping that isn't drawn? Does the sequence let the waterproofer finish before the framing crew starts puncturing the surface with temporary bracing and anchors?

Those are three disciplines' drawings, usually landscape, architectural, and structural, describing one assembly. They are rarely overlaid until someone is standing on a wet deck.

Check 5: Differential movement in the details that cross the line

Wood shrinks as it dries and the structure settles, and the concrete or steel below does not move the same way. Anything rigid that crosses the transition has to be detailed for that difference or it will bind, crack, or pull loose. In constructability terms the question is whether the detail can actually be built as drawn, in the order the trades arrive.

The review looks at elements that span both systems:

  • Stair and elevator shaft walls, where the lower enclosure is concrete or masonry and the upper is framed.
  • Exterior cladding attachment and flashing across the transition.
  • Plumbing and fire protection risers with fixed connections at both ends, which need an offset, a flexible connection, or a sleeve with clearance to absorb movement.
  • Finishes and door frames that straddle the interface.

The specifics depend on the structure and the products selected, so the review confirms that a movement detail exists, that it's coordinated across the sheets that reference it, and that the specification calls for compatible products.

Why these checks fall between the disciplines

Each design firm reviews its own sheets. The structural engineer's quality control confirms the structural set is internally consistent. The architect's confirms the architectural set. Neither is scoped to ask whether the concrete contractor will have the framer's anchor layout before the pour, or whether the sprinkler contractor's sleeves will be drawn in time. That's the same gap we describe in what a constructability review consultant checks that a cost estimator doesn't: an estimator prices what's drawn, and a constructability review asks what the drawings leave undefined.

On a podium project the gap is wider than usual because the interface is a schedule handoff as well as a drawing handoff. Wood framing cannot start until the deck is done, so a deck correction after the fact costs time on a critical sequence as well as money.

How to evaluate a consultant for this work

If you are choosing a reviewer for a podium set, the proposal should name the deck interface as an explicit scope item, not fold it into a generic coordination pass. Ask whether the reviewer will read the concrete, wood-frame, MEP, fire protection, and waterproofing sheets against each other at the deck, and whether the findings are located by sheet and ranked by consequence. The red flags in a weak constructability review proposal are worth reading before you sign.

Key takeaways

  • On a podium job, a constructability review concentrates on the deck handoff between concrete and wood, where five or more trades need information from each other.
  • Anything cast into the deck, including anchors, hold-downs, and sleeves, has to be fully defined before the pour, or it becomes field-drilled work.
  • Deferred submittals for trusses, sprinklers, and lateral systems each force decisions on the deck; unassigned "by others" notes are open scope for every bidder.
  • Waterproofing and drainage at the deck are drawn by several disciplines and rarely overlaid until water shows up.
  • Ask that the reviewer's scope names the deck interface explicitly and reads all trades' sheets against each other there.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a constructability review consultant do on a podium project?

The consultant reads the drawings and specifications for what can and can't be built as drawn, with particular attention to the interface between the podium and the wood frame above. That means checking tolerances, anchorage, deferred-submittal timing, waterproofing continuity, and movement details across all trades, then reporting each conflict by sheet.

When should a podium set be reviewed?

Before bid, when a correction can go out as an addendum that every bidder prices. A second look at the deck-level drawings before the podium pour is also valuable, because that's the last point where cast-in items can be changed without field work.

How is this different from a code review of the podium?

Code review confirms the building meets requirements such as fire-resistance ratings and construction type limits. Constructability review asks whether the trades can build it, in sequence, with the information the drawings give them. The two overlap at the fire-rated assembly but answer different questions.

Who owns the coordination between the concrete and wood-frame drawings?

Often nobody does by default. The structural engineer, the wood-frame designer, the architect, and the MEP engineers each own their own sheets, and the general contractor coordinates in the field, which is after the deck is poured. An independent review is one way to put the coordination before bid rather than after award.

Does a podium project need a review if the architect already ran QC?

Architect QC checks the architect's set. A review across disciplines checks how the structural, MEP, fire protection, and waterproofing sheets line up against each other, which is a separate scope. See what architect QC covers and where an owner-side review picks up.
