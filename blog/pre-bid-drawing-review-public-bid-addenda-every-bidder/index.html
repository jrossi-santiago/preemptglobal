Pre-Bid Drawing Review for Public Bid Projects: What Changes When Addenda Have to Go to Every Bidder

On a private project, a set can be corrected quietly. On a public bid, every correction is a formal addendum, issued to every plan holder, on a clock. That makes the window before the bid set goes out the cheapest place to fix anything.

Founding Principal

On a public bid project, the set can't be quietly corrected. Once it is advertised, every change goes out as a written addendum to every registered plan holder, and every bidder has to acknowledge it. That turns pre-bid drawing review into a different job than it is on a negotiated project: the review isn't tuning a set before one contractor prices it, it is the last chance to get the set right before the entire bidding field prices the same mistakes at once.

Why addenda are the only correction tool on a public bid

Public procurement rules are built so that no bidder gets information another bidder doesn't. A clarification given to one contractor in a phone call is a procurement problem. So the fix for an error in the bid set is an addendum, issued to everyone on the plan holder list, and typically referenced on the bid form.

That structure has three consequences owners should plan around:

  • Timing is regulated, and it varies. Many public owners require addenda to reach plan holders a minimum time before bid opening. The University of California facilities manual, for example, calls for addenda to be received no later than three business days before the bid deadline, while other jurisdictions set seven days or as little as 48 hours. Check the rule that governs your project; it sets how late a correction can still be made.
  • Acknowledgment is a responsiveness issue. Bidders are generally required to acknowledge every addendum on the bid form, and failing to do so can make a bid non-responsive. A flood of late addenda raises the odds that someone misses one.
  • The bid date may have to move. When an addendum is substantial or lands close to opening, the owner often has to extend the deadline so bidders can price it. Every extension is schedule the owner didn't plan on spending.
Worth knowing

Addendum deadlines differ by owner and jurisdiction. Confirm the minimum issue window in your own procurement documents before deciding when a review has to be finished. The review date should be backed out from that deadline, not from the advertised bid date.

What a bad set costs when it reaches every bidder at once

On a negotiated project, a conflict found after the contractor is selected becomes a conversation. On a public bid, a conflict found after advertisement becomes one of three things.

An addendum that arrives late. The correction is issued close to opening, bidders price it under pressure, and the spread between bids widens because each carries a different contingency for the part they couldn't fully price.

An addendum that isn't issued. The conflict goes undiscovered until after award. The low bidder priced one reading of the set, the owner expects another, and the difference is a change order. The low-bid mechanics behind that are covered in change order risk mitigation on public bid projects.

A conflict every bidder carried as a contingency. Not every unclear detail triggers an RFI. Some bidders price the unknown into a number the owner never sees itemized, and the public record shows only a total.

In all three, the cost of the error is paid by the owner, because the owner issued the set.

How the review changes on a public bid

The checking itself is the same cross-discipline work: drawings against specifications, structure against MEP, architectural against civil. What changes is the plan around it.

Finish earlier than feels necessary

A review of the full set takes 48 hours per pass at Preempt Global, but the findings then need to be resolved by the design team, and any resulting revisions need to be re-checked. On a public bid, that has to all happen before the set is advertised or, at the latest, in time to go out as an addendum inside the required window. The practical rule is to put the review ahead of the advertisement, not between advertisement and opening. The general logic is the same as in pre-bid vs. IFC review timing, but the penalty for being late is higher.

Batch corrections instead of drip-feeding them

Every addendum is a document bidders must read, reconcile with their takeoff, and acknowledge. Five small addenda cost bidders more attention than one consolidated one. A review that produces a single, sheet-located list of conflicts, ranked by severity, lets the design team issue one clean correction rather than a stream of patches.

Write findings so they translate directly into addendum language

Addenda have to be unambiguous because they are contractual. A finding that says "sheet M-201 shows a 24-inch duct crossing the beam at gridline C, which S-301 shows as W18 at the same elevation" can be turned into an addendum item with a revised sheet. A finding that says "possible clash near the mechanical room" cannot.

Re-review the revised set

Addenda change the set, and changes create new conflicts. A revision to a structural detail may shift an opening that an electrical drawing relied on. When addenda are issued, the revised sheets should be checked against the rest of the set, not just against the item they were meant to fix.

Who should own the timeline

Public agencies often run advertisement on a fixed cycle tied to board approvals, funding dates, or construction seasons. That makes the pre-bid review a scheduling question as much as a technical one. Owner's reps and project managers should set the review date by working backward from the addenda deadline, then leave room for one round of design team revisions.

This is also where procurement requirements affect what an outside reviewer can do. Confidentiality, access to the sealed set, and conflict-of-interest rules vary by agency; what changes for public agency procurement covers those in more detail. Reviewers are not part of the bidding field, so independence from bidders is built into the arrangement.

Key takeaways

  • On a public bid, the only way to correct the set after advertisement is an addendum, issued to every plan holder.
  • Addendum timing is regulated and varies by owner; one major university system, for example, requires receipt three business days before bid opening.
  • Bidders generally must acknowledge every addendum, so late or numerous addenda raise the risk of non-responsive bids and wider price spreads.
  • The cheapest correction window is before advertisement, when the set can be revised without any addendum.
  • Sheet-located, severity-ranked findings convert cleanly into addendum language; vague findings don't.

A public bid is a one-shot pricing event. The set that goes out is the set every bidder prices, and the review that matters is the one finished before it does.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an addendum in a public bid?

An addendum is a formal written change to the bid documents issued after advertisement. It is sent to every registered plan holder so all bidders price the same information, and bidders typically must acknowledge it on the bid form.

How far before bid opening do addenda have to be issued?

It depends on the owner and jurisdiction. Some set the minimum at 48 hours, others at three business days, and others at seven days. The governing procurement rules or bid instructions state the requirement for a given project.

What happens if a bidder doesn't acknowledge an addendum?

Many public owners treat a missed acknowledgment as a responsiveness issue, and the bid may be rejected as non-responsive. The exact rule depends on the owner's bid instructions and applicable law.

Can pre-bid drawing review remove the need for addenda?

It can reduce them. Conflicts found and corrected before the set is advertised never need an addendum. Errors found after advertisement still do, which is why finishing the review before advertisement is the cheaper path.
