When to Hire a Drawing Review Consultant on a Design-Assist or Progressive Design-Build Project

The usual advice is to review before bid. On delivery methods where the set is issued in pieces and the price gets locked mid-design, the window moves. Here's where it lands.

JR
Joe Rossi
Founding Principal
September 30, 2026·11 min read

The standard answer to "when to hire a drawing review consultant" is a phase between a late design milestone and the bid date. That answer assumes there is a bid date, and a finished set to point at. Design-assist and progressive design-build projects often have neither. The set is issued in packages, trade input is folded in while drawings are still moving, and the price commitment can be made before the design is complete. The question for an owner on those delivery methods isn't "before bid or after," it's "which of several decision points is the one where an independent set of eyes changes the outcome."

Why the usual timing advice breaks on these delivery methods

In design-bid-build, the timing guide is simple because the project has a hard sequence: design finishes, the set is issued for bid, contractors price it, and the owner signs. Every coordination conflict still sitting in the set at issuance gets priced into the bid, discovered as an RFI, or resolved later as a change order. The window for an independent review is the stretch before issuance, and it closes when the set goes out. We covered that version in our general timing guide for when an owner should hire a drawing review consultant.

Design-assist and progressive design-build reorder that sequence:

  • Design-assist brings trade contractors, often mechanical, electrical, curtain wall, or structural steel, into design before the drawings are final. The base design team still issues the documents, but trade input is captured along the way.
  • Progressive design-build engages one entity for design and construction early, typically on qualifications. Design advances with the owner involved, and a price commitment, often a guaranteed maximum price or lump sum, is set partway through rather than at the end of a finished design.

Both methods share the same structural change. There's no single moment when a complete set exists and the owner is about to commit. Commitments are made in stages against a design that is still changing. That's why the review question shifts from "is the set ready to bid" to "is what we're about to commit to actually coordinated."

Worth knowing

On a delivery method with staged commitments, the right time to hire a drawing review consultant is set by the commitment points, not by a calendar milestone. Anywhere the owner is about to lock scope, price, or a package release against drawings that haven't been reviewed across disciplines is a candidate window.

The decision points that matter

Instead of one window, there are several. They don't all matter equally on every project, but each is a moment when the cost of a coordination conflict changes character.

Before early packages releaseLong-lead and early-work packages get bought and built against drawings that won't be revisited

Before the price is locked

This is the highest-leverage point on a progressive design-build project. Once a guaranteed maximum price or lump sum is set, a coordination conflict found afterward gets sorted out inside that commitment: through contingency, through scope negotiation, or through a change to the agreement. Finding it before the commitment means the conflict gets resolved in the drawings, where fixing it costs a revision rather than a negotiation.

Owners on progressive design-build often have less independent visibility at this moment than they'd have on a bid-set project, because the design team and the builder are one relationship and the set the price is based on is still evolving. An independent review at this point isn't a judgment on that team's work. It's a second reading of whether the documents the price rests on agree with themselves across architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing, and between drawings and specs.

Before early packages are released

Staged delivery means some work gets bought and built before the rest of the design is finished: site work, structural steel, foundations, long-lead equipment. Those packages are drawn against assumptions about what other disciplines will do later. If the assumption is wrong, the package can't be quietly revised the way an unissued drawing can. The steel is fabricated, the equipment is ordered, the slab is poured.

A review timed to the early package release checks the package against everything else that has been drawn so far, and flags where a later discipline's likely needs conflict with what's being locked in. That's a narrower review than a full-set audit, and it can be scoped that way.

After trade input lands on sheets

On design-assist, a trade partner's advice is valuable, but advice in a meeting is not the same as advice correctly reflected on an issued sheet and still consistent with the rest of the set. We walked through that gap in why early trade input doesn't eliminate the review step, and the timing implication is direct: the point to bring in an independent reviewer is after the trade input has been drawn, not while it's still being discussed. Reviewing too early checks drawings that are about to change. Reviewing after checks whether the input survived being put on paper.

At major revisions

Every project has moments when something large changes: an owner-driven program shift, a value engineering round, a structural revision, a change to the mechanical concept. Each one re-opens areas the team had already treated as settled. On staged delivery, those revisions often happen after some commitments are already in place, which raises the cost of missing a downstream conflict. A review triggered by the revision, scoped to the affected disciplines, is often more useful than a full pass at the end.

What's different about hiring a reviewer on these projects

A few practical points separate this from a conventional single-review engagement.

Retainer structure tends to fit better than a single review. When there are multiple decision points across a project, a reviewer engaged once sees one snapshot. A reviewer engaged across the project sees how the set changed between snapshots, which is where most staged-delivery conflicts originate. Whether a single review or a standing arrangement makes sense depends on how many commitment points the project has and how much the design is expected to move between them.

Scope each review to the decision, not the whole set. A review ahead of an early steel package and a review ahead of price lock ask different questions. Say which decision the review supports, and the reviewer can prioritize the interfaces that decision depends on.

Keep the review independent of the team producing the drawings. The reason to bring in a third party isn't distrust of the design team or the builder. It's that a team that produced and coordinated the drawings is the least likely to see what they've stopped looking at. An outside reader with no stake in how a conflict got there is faster at spotting it. Done well, it makes everyone's job easier, including the architect's and the builder's, because conflicts get resolved as drawing revisions instead of field problems.

Get the timing into the schedule early. Independent review turnaround needs to fit inside the design schedule without extending it. At Preempt Global, a review pass runs 48 hours, so it can be slotted immediately ahead of a commitment point without moving the date. The mistake to avoid is deciding a review is needed the week before the price is due.

A simple test for whether you're in a review window

Ask these questions at each stage of a design-assist or progressive design-build project:

  1. Is the owner about to commit to a price, a package, or a scope that depends on drawings no one has read across disciplines?
  2. Has any trade or design partner given input that's supposed to be reflected on the sheets, and has anyone confirmed it is?
  3. Has something significant changed since the last time the affected areas were checked against each other?
  4. Would fixing a conflict in this area be cheaper now than after the next commitment?

If the answer to any of those is yes, it's a review window. If the answer to all of them is yes, it's probably the most valuable window on the project.

Key takeaways

  • Design-assist and progressive design-build don't produce a single bid set, so timing a review to bid date doesn't work.
  • The strongest window is before the price or scope commitment is locked, when conflicts can still be fixed in the drawings.
  • Early package releases and major revisions are secondary windows, each with its own narrower scope.
  • Review design-assist input after it's been drawn on the sheets, not while it's still being discussed.
  • Multiple decision points make a standing arrangement more useful than a single review on many staged projects.

The owners who get the most from independent review on these delivery methods are the ones who stop asking "when is the set done" and start asking "what am I about to commit to, and has anyone outside the team read it." That question has an answer at every stage of the project, and the review belongs wherever the answer is no. Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to hire a drawing review consultant on a progressive design-build project?

Before the price commitment, whether that's a guaranteed maximum price or a lump sum, is locked. After that point, a coordination conflict gets resolved inside the commitment through contingency or negotiation rather than through a drawing revision. Early package releases and major design revisions are additional points worth reviewing.

Is there a bid set to review on a design-assist project?

Often not in the traditional sense. Design-assist projects typically issue drawings in stages while trade input is still being incorporated, so there may be no single finished set before commitments start. The review timing follows the commitment points and the moments when trade input is captured on the sheets.

Should a drawing review consultant be hired before or after the trade partners give their input?

After the input is drawn, not before. Reviewing before trade input is captured checks drawings that are about to change. Reviewing after checks whether the input made it onto the issued sheets correctly and still agrees with the rest of the set.

Does hiring an independent reviewer imply the design team or builder isn't doing their job?

No. A team that produced and coordinated the drawings is the party least positioned to see what they've stopped looking at, and an outside reader with no stake in how a conflict got there is faster at finding it. Resolving conflicts as drawing revisions makes the architect's and the builder's jobs easier, not harder.

Does an independent review slow down a design-assist or progressive design-build schedule?

It shouldn't if it's planned. A 48-hour review pass can be slotted directly ahead of a commitment point without moving the date. The delay comes from deciding a review is needed at the last minute, not from the review itself.
