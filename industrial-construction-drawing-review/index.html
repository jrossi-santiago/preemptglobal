Why Industrial Sets Carry More Risk

THE BUILDING IS DESIGNED AROUND EQUIPMENT THAT ISN'T FULLY SPEC'D YET. THAT'S THE RISK.

A standard construction document review checks submittals, spec-to-drawing alignment, code compliance, and MEP coordination — and an industrial set needs every bit of that. What's layered on top is a building designed around equipment that's often still a preliminary cut sheet when the structural and utility drawings get issued, process piping and utility systems as dense as any MEP set, and — on a brownfield project — a tie-in to a facility that's still running production while it's being built onto.

Equipment-Driven Design

Vendor-specified foundations, anchor bolts, and utility connections that can still change after the structural set is drawn.

Brownfield Tie-Ins To A Live Facility

New structure and utilities connecting into an existing, often still-operating plant — tie-ins that can't just be redesigned around.

Fast-Track Tied To A Production Date

Schedules locked to a production or revenue date, not just a general contract date — a missed conflict competes with that deadline.

What Gets Checked On An Industrial Set

EVERY SHEET, INCLUDING THE ONE BUILT AROUND THE EQUIPMENT.

The issued set is read once, in full, by reviewers who've drawn these sets themselves — not a keyword search, not a single-discipline pass. On an industrial set, four things get checked specifically:

EQUIPMENT & FOUNDATIONS

Vendor equipment specs — foundations, anchor bolts, utility connections — cross-checked against the structural and MEP drawings before a footing is poured to the wrong dimension.

PROCESS PIPING & UTILITIES

Compressed air, process gas, chemical lines, and high-voltage power routing cross-checked against structural and mechanical sheets for clearance conflicts.

CRANE & CONVEYOR TOLERANCES

Crane rail, conveyor, and high-bay clear-height dimensions checked sheet to sheet — small errors compound across long runs.

BROWNFIELD TIE-INS

New structure and utility tie-in points checked against the existing facility's as-built conditions and the phasing plan protecting live production.

The output is a written findings report — every conflict located by sheet and detail, severity-ranked (High/Med/Low), with an estimated dollar exposure and schedule impact on each — plus a live walkthrough meeting. Before You Ask

STILL GOT QUESTIONS?
HERE'S THE STRAIGHT ANSWER.

What makes industrial construction drawing review different from a standard construction document review? + –

The method is the same — an independent, owner-side check of the issued set. What's different is what's driving conflicts: equipment vendor specs that arrive or change late, process piping and utility systems layered into the structure, and — on many projects — a tie-in to an existing, still-operating facility instead of a clean site.

Why do vendor equipment specs create so much coordination risk? + –

Because foundations, anchor bolts, and utility connections often get drawn to a preliminary equipment cut sheet before the vendor issues final dimensions — and final dimensions can shift. A review checks whether the structural and MEP sheets still match the equipment that's actually being installed, not just the placeholder it started as.

What's a brownfield tie-in, and why does it need its own check? + –

A brownfield tie-in is new construction connecting into an existing, often still-operating facility — a utility tie-in, a structural connection, or a phased sequence that has to keep production running during construction. Those tie-in points depend on as-built conditions that don't always match the original drawings, which is exactly where an independent review earns its keep.

Doesn't BIM clash detection already catch this? + –

It catches a meaningful share — modeled elements overlapping in 3D. It's less reliable on industrial sets where equipment is still modeled as a vendor placeholder block awaiting final dimensions, or where the existing facility being tied into was never modeled at all. This review reads the issued documents themselves, including the notes a placeholder block can't carry. Full comparison →

When should this happen relative to a production start date? + –

As early as there's an issued set — pre-bid or at IFC — since industrial schedules are often locked to a production or revenue date that doesn't move. Catching a conflict before equipment orders and structural steel are committed keeps that date intact; catching it after means the fix competes with the same deadline. Pre-bid vs. IFC review timing →

What kinds of industrial projects need this? + –

Manufacturing plants, warehouse and distribution centers, process and chemical facilities, and other industrial builds with multiple trades and equipment vendors sharing tight coordination — new construction or a brownfield addition to a live facility. There's no minimum construction value.
