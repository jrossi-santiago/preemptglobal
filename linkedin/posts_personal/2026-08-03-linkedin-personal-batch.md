Day 1 — Doc

Your submittal log has line items sitting past 21 days with nobody assigned to chase them. That's not a documentation problem — it's a schedule problem wearing a documentation costume.

The Submittal Log Triage Framework fixes it with three tiers, checked weekly:

Green: reviewed and returned inside the contract turnaround (usually 10-15 business days). No action needed.

Yellow: past turnaround, no response yet. Flag it to the PM by name — not a colored cell in a spreadsheet nobody opens. Someone owns it by end of day.

Red: past 21 days with no movement. Treat it as a procurement risk, not a paperwork backlog. Long-lead items sitting in this bucket start eating float before anyone notices, because the order can't go in until the submittal's approved.

I ran this on a 140-line submittal log last month. Fourteen items were sitting in red, three of them long-lead MEP gear with 16-week lead times already baked into the schedule. Nobody had escalated a single one.

Triage isn't about reviewing faster. It's about knowing which stalled item is quietly costing you weeks, and which one is just paperwork waiting its turn.

Pull your log this week and sort it into these three tiers. If your red bucket has anything long-lead sitting in it, that's your Monday morning call.

Day 2 — Doc

Your pay application says 62% complete. Your cost-loaded schedule says 54%. Somebody's wrong, and if it's the contractor, you just over-approved a draw.

The Pay Application Cross-Check is three lines run against every G702/G703 before signature:

One — percent-complete on the application versus percent-complete on the schedule, checked by cost code, not just the project total. A project can look 60% done in aggregate while one critical cost code is stalled at 20%.

Two — stored materials on-site versus the invoice backing it up. Materials billed but not on-site or in a bonded warehouse is the single most common overbilling move I see on these applications.

Three — retainage math, recalculated independently, not copied from the contractor's own number. A 10% retainage error on a $2M draw is $200K sitting in the wrong column.

Caught before signature, it's a phone call and a corrected application. Caught at closeout, it's a retainage dispute with a contractor who's already demobilized and has no incentive to reconcile anything with you.

Run these three checks against your next application before you sign it. Takes fifteen minutes, and it's the fifteen minutes that keeps you from funding work that hasn't actually happened yet.

Day 3 — Story

A GC hit rock two feet below the geotechnical report's assumed elevation and filed a $180K differing site conditions claim. The daily logs decided it — and not in the direction anyone expected.

The owner's PM had been requiring photo-dated daily logs from the excavation sub since notice to proceed, mostly out of habit. When the claim landed, those logs showed exactly when the rock was encountered, what equipment was on site that day, and how long the crew stood down waiting for a change directive. The claim resolved in eleven days instead of the usual months-long fight, because there was no ambiguity left for either side to argue.

Compare that to a project I reviewed later that year: same claim type, no daily logs, just a demand letter six weeks after the fact reconstructing dates from memory. That one took four months and settled for more than it should have, because the owner had no contemporaneous record to push back with.

Differing site conditions claims aren't won on the merits of the condition. They're won on whether someone was writing down what happened while it was happening.

If your excavation and utility subs aren't logging daily with photos, that's the gap to close before your next claim lands, not after.

Day 4 — Story

A subcontractor's indemnification clause got redlined during a rushed contract turnaround, and nobody caught it until a $340K injury claim landed on the owner's desk instead of the sub's insurer.

The original AIA-based subcontract had standard mutual indemnification language. Somewhere between the third and final draft, a redline narrowed it to the sub's "sole negligence" only — which meant any claim involving shared fault, even 5% owner exposure, shifted the whole defense burden back up the chain. Nobody flagged it, because nobody read the final executed version against the original scope. They compared it to the prior draft, not to what the parties actually agreed to.

I've seen this exact pattern on three separate projects now: risk allocation language drifts during redlines, and the drift never gets checked against the original intent, only against the last version someone happens to remember.

The fix isn't more legal review. It's a specific check: read the final signed indemnification and insurance sections cold, with no memory of the negotiation, and ask what claim scenario would fall outside them.

Before your next subcontract executes, do that read. It's twenty minutes against a six-figure gap nobody will notice until a claim finds it for you.

Day 5 — Midas

$28,400 removed from a pay application before it ever reached the owner's desk — because one cost code got billed twice under two different line-item descriptions.

Here's what happened: the electrical cost code for rough-in was billed as complete in month four. In month five's application, the same scope reappeared under "electrical trim," with 40% of the same rough-in work re-described and re-billed. Different words, same conduit runs, same panel feeds. On a 90-line schedule of values, it's an easy miss — nobody's reading line-item descriptions closely enough to catch two rows pointing at the same physical work.

The catch came from one habit: cross-referencing every cost code against the prior month's application before approving the current one, instead of checking the current month in isolation.

That single habit has caught double-billing on four different projects this year, averaging somewhere between $15K and $40K each time. It's rarely fraud, in my experience — mostly sloppy SOV breakdowns and rushed billing cycles. But sloppy costs the owner the same as intentional does if nobody's checking.

Pull last month's application next to this month's before you approve the next one. Fifteen minutes, cost code by cost code, before the money moves.

Day 6 — Conviction

Value engineering that only cuts and never re-verifies isn't engineering. It's a budget haircut wearing an engineer's title.

Real value engineering asks two questions in order: what does this change cost, and what does it change downstream — in coordination, in code compliance, in the next trade's scope. Most VE exercises I see only ever ask the first question. A spec gets downgraded, an assembly gets simplified, a system gets value-engineered from redundant to single-path — and nobody re-runs the coordination pass to confirm the change doesn't collide with something three sheets away.

I watched a VE swap on a rated corridor assembly save $40K on paper and then cost $95K in rework six months later, because the substitution changed the fire rating and nobody re-checked it against the code summary. The VE log showed a clean cost reduction. Nobody ever closed the loop on whether the building still worked the way it was supposed to.

Cutting cost isn't the hard part. Cutting cost without breaking something you already coordinated is the hard part, and it's the part most VE processes skip entirely.

If your VE log doesn't have a re-verification column next to the savings column, it isn't tracking value. It's just tracking cuts.

Day 7 — Transformation

Before: closeout is a scramble. The GC's PM is chasing eleven subs for as-builts three weeks after substantial completion, half the O&M manuals are missing startup reports, and the owner's facilities team inherits a building with documentation gaps nobody notices until the first work order.

After: closeout starts at 50% construction complete, not after it. A QA/QC closeout checklist gets built into every subcontract from day one — as-builts due with each pay application instead of bundled at the end, O&M manual components logged against a master index as equipment gets started up, warranty start dates captured the day of activation instead of reconstructed from memory eight months later.

The difference isn't effort. It's timing. The scramble version and the checklist version take roughly the same total hours of documentation work — the checklist version just does it in small pieces while the information is still fresh, instead of all at once when everyone's demobilized and half the answers live in an inbox that's already been archived.

If your last three closeouts all felt like a scramble, the fix isn't hiring more closeout staff. It's moving the same work earlier and spreading it out.

Start the checklist at 50% complete on your next project, not at substantial completion.
