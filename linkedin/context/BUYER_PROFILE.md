Refine it with a real anecdote or direct quote the next time one becomes available — specificity from an actual conversation always beats a composite.

## Who this buyer actually is

Not "the construction industry." Not "project managers." The buyer is one of a few specific roles, on a project big enough and coordinated-enough (multi-discipline, tight tolerances) that a missed clash is expensive:

- An **owner or owner's rep** carrying the schedule and budget risk directly.
- A **GC principal or project executive** who eats the change order politically even when it's not contractually their fault.
- An **A/E partner** whose firm's name is on the set when something doesn't coordinate.

This is not a $1,375 one-off buyer. The resolution document is written for the buyer who retains: Simple Retainer at $2,750/mo or Custom Retainer starting at $5,000/mo (uncapped with add-ons), across a full project cycle. A single quarter of that relationship runs $8,250 to $15,000+ before any add-ons or multiple concurrent projects — the same order of magnitude `STRATEGY.md`'s illustrative "$8k-$25k this quarter" framing describes, reached through Preempt's real pricing rather than the source material's numbers.

## His Tuesday

It's 4pm. He's not looking at the drawing set - he's looking at a submittal log with an item stuck past its third touch, no owner assigned, and a text from a sub asking when the fire-rated wall detail is coming back. He doesn't know yet whether that's a nothing or a $40,000 problem. He won't know for another three weeks, when it's either resolved quietly or it's a change order with his name near it.

He's had the other version of this Tuesday before. Last time it was a beam and a duct wanting the same six inches at Level 4 - found in the field, not on paper, which meant a stop-work conversation, an 18-day slip, and a phone call to the people funding the project that he'd rather not make twice. Everyone on that call said some version of "we'll tighten up coordination going forward." Nobody could say what specifically would change.

He has a team that tells him the current set is clean. He's not sure that team would catch it if it weren't - they drew it, and nobody catches their own blind spot at the rate an outsider does. He's tired of finding out three weeks after the set already went to build, at field-cost pricing, instead of finding out on paper, at the cost of one redline and five minutes.

He is not going to say any of this out loud in a meeting. He is going to read an archive of posts at 2am over a few weeks, checking whether the person writing them still sounds like the same person three months apart, before he ever sends a message.

## What makes a post land at his resolution (vs. a generic industry post)

Generic: "Change orders are a huge problem in construction."
Tuesday-resolution: "Your submittal log has an item stuck past touch three right now, and nobody owns moving it. That's not a hard item. That's a process failure with a name."

Generic: "QA/QC catches issues before they become expensive."
Tuesday-resolution: "$42,000 and an 18-day slip - a beam and a duct that wanted the same six inches at Level 4, found in the field instead of on paper. That's the version of this Tuesday you're trying to avoid."

The difference is always the same move: name the specific artifact he's actually looking at (the log, the redline, the punch list, the dashboard, the text from the sub) and the specific number attached to getting it wrong, instead of describing the problem category from a distance.

## The 200, not the 40,000

Every post is a filter. If a post could be nodded along to by anyone who's ever worked construction, it's too wide - a resident, a sub, a PM at a firm too small to ever retain. Rewrite until it only fully lands for someone carrying schedule/budget risk on a multi-discipline set large enough that a missed clash costs five figures. Everyone else feeling nothing is not a failure of the post - it's the design working correctly.
