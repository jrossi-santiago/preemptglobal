Day 1 — Full-System Giveaway

Pull this week's three-week look-ahead and lay it next to the issued set. Not the schedule software's model of the set. The actual sheets.

Most GCs update the look-ahead every week without fail. Almost none of them re-check it against the set it's supposed to be built from. The look-ahead moves fast: activities get resequenced, durations get compressed to recover a slipped week, trades get reshuffled to keep the crew count busy. The set underneath it doesn't move at the same speed. An addendum lands, a submittal gets revised, and the activity on the look-ahead still assumes the version from two weeks ago.

I run every active set through what I call the Look-Ahead Lock. Three steps, every week the look-ahead updates.

Step one: pull the current three-week look-ahead and the current issued set side by side, not from memory, not from the last time someone checked.

Step two: for every activity due to start inside the window, confirm the drawing or spec section it depends on hasn't been revised since the activity was sequenced. If it has, that activity doesn't get to keep its start date until someone confirms the revision doesn't change the work.

Step three: flag the mismatch to the person who owns the schedule, by activity name and sheet number, before the crew shows up. Not after.

Here's the terrain. Step two is the one that gets skipped under pressure, because most weeks nothing's changed and it feels like busywork. The week something did change is indistinguishable from every other week until someone actually looks. Skip the check for a month straight and the first sign of trouble is a crew standing on site with the wrong version in hand.

Across the sets we run this on, float doesn't usually disappear in one big schedule event. It leaks out three days at a time, every time a look-ahead assumed a set that had already changed underneath it.

Most GCs have a look-ahead. Very few of them have a lock step tying it back to the actual set.

Day 2 — Case Study Breakdown

A G703 continuation sheet claiming 60 percent complete on a system that hadn't cleared submittal review yet. That's the finding that held up a pay application, not a missing signature.

The G702 summary looked routine: percentages moving in line with the schedule, nothing that would catch a reviewer skimming totals. The problem was underneath it, on the continuation sheet, where the billed percentage for the specialty electrical gear assumed equipment that was still sitting in submittal review, unapproved, with two open comments the sub hadn't answered yet. Whoever built the schedule of values was tracking progress against the install schedule, not against what had actually cleared paper.

We caught it on paper, cross-referencing the submittal log against the billed percentages line by line, before the application went to the owner for payment.

Left uncaught, that's an owner paying for progress that isn't real, a retainage dispute waiting for the day someone finally checks, and a change order conversation buried inside what should have been a routine draw.

12% of total project budget gets lost to change orders on average. A good share of that starts exactly here, in a pay application nobody cross-checked against the log that actually governs it.

Day 3 — Short-Form High-Signal

Every change order meeting I've sat in spends its first twenty minutes on markup percentage. Ten and five, fifteen and ten, whatever the contract allows, argued line by line.

Nobody in that room is arguing about whether the change should have existed at all. On most of the sets we've reviewed, the coordination gap driving the change order was sitting in the issued set before the sub ever picked up a tool. The negotiation everyone shows up for is about who pays what percentage on top of a number that shouldn't have been a number in the first place.

Markup negotiation is downstream. The real fight was on paper, weeks earlier, and nobody showed up for it.

Day 4 — Full-System Giveaway

Somewhere in your contract file is a performance bond, and if you ever have to call on it, the surety isn't going to take your word for why.

Bond claims get denied more often on the documentation than on the underlying default. A sub can be genuinely behind, genuinely underperforming, exactly the situation the bond was written to cover, and the claim still dies because the record built around it can't survive being read by a surety's claims adjuster who wasn't on site for any of it.

I run every set with a bond in play through what I call the Default Notice Record. Three steps, starting the moment performance becomes a real question, not a suspicion.

Step one: document the specific failure against the specific contract requirement it violates, by section, not "the sub is behind." A missed submittal deadline, an unresolved RFI past the contractually required response window, a schedule milestone missed with no recovery plan filed. Each one tied to the clause it breaches.

Step two: notice, in writing, inside the exact window the bond and the contract both specify. Most owners assume the bond company will figure out the timeline later. The bond doesn't work that way. Miss the notice window and the surety has a clean argument that the claim wasn't preserved, regardless of how bad the underperformance actually was.

Step three: keep pricing the impact as it accrues, not reconstructed after the fact. A findings report with a dollar exposure attached to each item, dated as the items were found, is a different document to a claims adjuster than a summary written the week the claim gets filed.

Here's the terrain. Step three is the one that saves the claim, and it's the one nobody's tracking until they need it. By the time a default gets declared, everyone wants the paperwork retroactively, and a record assembled after the fact reads exactly like what it is.

$177 billion a year gets lost industry-wide to uncoordinated items and the disputes that follow them. A real share of that never gets recovered, not because the claim wasn't valid, but because nobody was building the file before they needed it.

Day 5 — Case Study Breakdown

An RFI marked closed for six weeks, and the drawing it touched was never actually updated to match the answer.

The RFI itself was handled well. Question asked inside two days of the conflict showing up, answered inside the contractual window, filed and logged like it should be. The mistake wasn't in the RFI process. It was in what happened after: the answer changed a duct routing around a structural member, the response got logged as resolved, and the revision never made it back onto the issued sheet. Everyone downstream kept working from the original drawing, because as far as the log was concerned, the question was closed.

We caught the gap on paper, cross-referencing the RFI log against the current issued set, before the duct got fabricated to the wrong dimension.

Left uncaught, that's a fabrication run against a drawing that was already wrong, a field fit-up that doesn't work, and a second RFI asking why the first answer never took.

76% of projects fall behind schedule due to a poor QA/QC process, and an RFI that closes on paper without ever closing on the drawing is exactly the kind of gap that number is describing.

Day 6 — Short-Form High-Signal

A drone survey tells you what got built. It doesn't tell you what should have been built.

We've looked at plenty of drone captures that confirmed field conditions matched the model beautifully, on a system that was never coordinated correctly on paper in the first place. The survey wasn't wrong. It was answering a different question than the one that mattered. Caught in the field means the drone did its job on schedule. Caught on paper means nobody needed the drone for that item at all.

A drone catches drift between the model and the field. It was never going to catch a clash that was already sitting in the drawings before anyone broke ground.

Day 7 — Personality

I read the contract before I read a single sheet, and I know that's not how most reviewers start.

The drawings tell you what's supposed to get built. They don't tell you which document wins when two of them disagree, or how long a party has to notice a conflict before they lose the right to claim it. That's all sitting in the general conditions, usually AIA A201 or whatever the project's version of it is, and most of the time nobody's read it since it got signed.

I read it first because it changes what I'm actually looking for. A drawing-versus-spec conflict on a project where the contract says drawings govern is a different finding than the same conflict where specs govern. Skip the contract and you're flagging mismatches without knowing which one actually wins.

The set tells you what was drawn. The contract tells you what the drawing's word is worth.
