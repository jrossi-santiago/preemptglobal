Day 1 — Full-System Giveaway

Pull your open RFI log right now and look at the date column, not the subject line.

Somewhere on it is an RFI sitting past 10 days with no response and no owner chasing it. Nobody flagged it as urgent, because on the day it was submitted it looked like a paint color question or a duct routing clarification. Three weeks later it's the reason a sub is standing around, and the fix costs more than the RFI itself ever would have.

I run every open RFI through the same system. I call it the RFI Aging Ledger. Three stages, no shortcuts.

Day 0 to 5: log and classify. Every RFI gets tagged the day it's submitted, by trade and by consequence. Does an open answer stop work, or just delay a decision. Those are two different clocks. Work-stopping RFIs get one named person attached to the answer, not a department, and that name is visible to the GC.

Day 6 to 10: the escalation touch. Anything still open gets a direct call, not another email into the same inbox that missed it the first time. Across the sets we've reviewed, a good share of the RFIs that die past day 10 die because the first email went to someone who'd already rolled off the project.

Day 11 and beyond: the cost conversation. Every RFI still open past day 11 gets priced, what one more day of silence costs the schedule, named in dollars, sent up the chain attached to that number instead of "still waiting on a response."

Here's the terrain nobody warns you about. This only works if someone owns the ledger daily, not weekly. Miss three days in a row and it silently reverts to a spreadsheet nobody opens. The classification step at day 0 is the first thing that gets skipped under pressure, and it's the one step that makes the whole system work. Skip it, and you're just making phone calls in a different order.

Most firms have an RFI log. Almost none of them have a clock running on it.

Day 2 — Case Study Breakdown

$78,900 in avoided field costs. 32 days of schedule protected. That's what came out of one reviewed set, and the finding that mattered most for the pay application wasn't the biggest number on the report. It was the smallest one nobody had looked at twice.

The panel schedule had stopped matching the one-line diagram four revisions back. Nobody caught it because nobody was checking the two documents against each other. Electrical owned the panel schedule, the one-line lived in a different discipline's binder, and both got issued as current.

Left alone, that mismatch shows up at the worst possible time: mid-construction, when the sub pulls the panel schedule to order gear and finds it doesn't match what got energized on the one-line. The resulting change order lands right in the middle of a pay application cycle. Now it's not just a cost dispute, it's a retainage argument, because nobody can agree which version was the set of record when the work got priced.

We caught it on paper, during an independent pass, before either document went to build. One redline. Attached to the same findings report as the rest of that $78,900.

The pay application that quarter closed without a single line item in dispute.

Day 3 — Short-Form High-Signal

Everyone's asking whether AI can replace a QA/QC reviewer. Wrong question.

AI can flag that a spec section references a drawing number that doesn't exist. It cannot tell you that a fire-rated wall got value-engineered out of a corridor for a reason nobody wrote down, or that a panel schedule quietly stopped matching the one-line four revisions ago because two disciplines never actually talked.

Software finds mismatches. It doesn't know which mismatch used to be a decision.

The gap between a keyword search and someone who's actually drawn these sets isn't going to close with a better model. It's going to close when someone hunts, instead of running a search.

Day 4 — Full-System Giveaway

Somewhere in your long-lead schedule is an order date that was set against a spec that's since been revised, and nobody re-ran the lead time when the revision went out.

That's not a hypothetical. Switchgear, curtain wall, generators, custom AHUs, anything with a lead time measured in months gets ordered early, against whatever spec is current on the day the order gets placed. Specs get revised constantly after that: a voltage change, a dimension change, an addendum nobody circulated past the trade that issued it. The order already went out. Nobody circles back to check if it still matches.

The system I run on every set with long-lead scope, I call the Procurement Lock Sequence.

Step one: flag every long-lead item at first review, before a single order gets placed. Not "electrical will handle it," a named list, cross-referenced against the spec section and the submittal log, so there's one document that says what got ordered against what revision.

Step two: lock the spec at order date. Whatever revision is current the day the PO goes out gets attached to that item permanently. Any addendum after that date gets flagged specifically against the items it could affect, not broadcast to the whole set.

Step three: re-check at every subsequent review pass. Every time we're back in a set, the long-lead list gets checked against the current spec, not the one from three revisions ago. If something moved, it gets flagged before the item ships, not after it arrives on site and doesn't fit.

The part that's brutal to actually run: step three only works if someone's back in the set on a real cadence, not whenever there's time. A schedule slip caused by coordination failures on a commercial project runs 3 to 5 weeks on average. Almost all of that time gets lost waiting to notice a mismatch that already happened weeks earlier.

Most GCs have a long-lead tracker. Very few of them have a lock step in it.

Day 5 — Case Study Breakdown

$42,000 and an 18-day schedule slip. That's the fix once a beam and a duct both got built for the same six inches at Level 4, a clash that should have died on paper for the cost of one redline.

Here's what actually happened on the schedule once it hit the field. The duct was already hung. The beam was already erected. Work stopped in that zone while the two trades and the GC worked out who was moving what, and the fix ate 18 days straight out of the float that was supposed to cover exactly this kind of surprise. Once the float's gone, the next delay isn't absorbed anymore. It's a claim, with a paper trail, and both sides start protecting themselves instead of finishing the floor.

The independent pass on that set ran before the duct was ever hung. A clash that size gets caught on paper in minutes once someone's actually looking for the seam between structural and mechanical, instead of trusting that each trade checked its own work.

No claim got filed on that floor, because there was nothing left to argue about. The float stayed intact for the delay that actually needed it three weeks later.

Day 6 — Short-Form High-Signal

Value engineering removes the line item. It doesn't remove the reason the line item was there.

Across every set with a VE pass in its history, the same pattern shows up: something got cut for cost, the reason it existed got cut along with it, and three revisions later nobody on the current team remembers there was ever a reason to check that spot again.

The line item was the symptom. The coordination problem it was solving is still sitting in the set, waiting for someone to re-discover it the expensive way.

Cutting the cost is easy. Cutting the memory of why it mattered is what actually costs you.

Day 7 — Personality

I don't trust a QA/QC reviewer who's never drawn the set they're reviewing.

Not because a credential doesn't mean anything. Because knowing where a duct run actually gets modeled versus where it gets forgotten isn't something you learn from a class. You learn it from having been the person who forgot it once, on a real set, with your name on the sheet.

That's the whole reason I built this the way I did. Every reviewer on our end has spent years producing these same drawings before they ever reviewed one. Not a certification. Not a tool. The years.

A rubber stamp doesn't require having drawn anything. Hunting for the mismatch that's about to become a change order does. I'd rather have five people who've been burned by their own drawings than fifty who've only ever audited someone else's.

That's not a scalable answer. It's the correct one.
