Day 1 — Full-System Giveaway

Somewhere in your closeout binder is a warranty clock that's already running, and nobody's written down when it started.

Most owners treat warranty as one date: substantial completion, stamped on the certificate, applied to the whole project like a single expiration sticker. It's not one date. Every spec section names its own trigger, and they don't match. A chiller's warranty might start on the manufacturer's start-up report. A roof membrane's might start on beneficial occupancy. A generator's might start the day the commissioning agent signs off, which is rarely the same week as substantial completion. Treat them all as one date and you lose coverage on whichever ones actually started earlier, without ever finding out until the first service call gets billed.

I run every set with warranted equipment through what I call the Warranty Trigger Ledger. Three steps.

Step one: at first review, pull the warranty clause out of every spec section, not just the general conditions. Name the specific trigger event in each one, word for word. Substantial completion and manufacturer start-up are not interchangeable, and a set will use both in the same document without flagging the difference.

Step two: one ledger, one line per system, trigger event and expiration date named next to it. Not a spreadsheet buried in a closeout folder. A live document someone actually opens.

Step three: recheck the ledger against the manufacturer's actual start-up report when it lands, not the date on the certificate of substantial completion. Start-up reports come in late almost every time, and the real trigger date is whatever's written on that report, not whatever date got assumed months earlier.

Here's the terrain. Step three is the one that rots. Start-up reports trail the schedule by weeks on a project that's already run 3 to 5 weeks behind from coordination failures alone, and by the time the report shows up, the ledger's been closed out and forgotten. Skip the recheck and you find out your warranty ran out early on the day you need it, not before.

Most closeout binders have a warranty section. Almost none of them have a clock running on it.

Day 2 — Case Study Breakdown

$78,900 in avoided field costs. 32 days of schedule protected. That number's from one reviewed set, and it's not one finding, it's a ranked report full of them. This one started in a site visit report, not a drawing.

The report read clean: duct installed per plan, corridor ceiling closed up on schedule. Nobody flagged anything, because nothing looked wrong from the field. The problem wasn't visible from the field. It was in the cross-reference nobody had run: the fire-rating schedule called for a damper at every duct penetration through that corridor's rated wall, and the mechanical spec never carried the callout through to the submittal. The site visit report couldn't catch it, because it was only checking what got built against what got drawn. Nobody was checking the drawing against the spec that governed it.

We ran that check on paper, during an independent pass, before the ceiling closed. One redline, one submittal revision, one damper added to an order that hadn't shipped yet.

Left uncaught, that's a failed inspection after the ceiling's already closed, a corridor reopened, and a fire-rating deficiency with the owner's name on the certificate of occupancy application.

The site visit report was right. It just wasn't asking the question that mattered.

Day 3 — Short-Form High-Signal

Every GC I talk to has a PM platform now. Procore, PlanGrid, Autodesk Build, pick one. What changed is where the documents live. What didn't change is whether anyone's checking them against each other.

A platform organizes a set. It doesn't cross-reference it. Nobody's software flags that a submittal marked approved in the log never actually got a stamp, or that two disciplines issued conflicting revisions the same week, because the tool isn't looking for that relationship. It's just storing both files correctly, side by side, both wrong.

Adoption isn't a process. It's a filing cabinet that syncs faster than the old one.

Day 4 — Full-System Giveaway

Somewhere on your active site, a sub has already hit something that wasn't in the bid documents, and the clock on your right to claim it started the moment they found it.

Differing site condition claims get lost more often on the paperwork than on the merits. The condition itself might be exactly what it looks like: rock where the geotech report said soil, groundwater where the boring log said dry. That doesn't matter if the record built around it can't survive being read by someone who wasn't there.

I run every DSC situation through what I call the Differing Condition Record. Three steps, in order, starting the day it's found.

Step one: document at discovery, before anyone touches the condition further. Photos from a fixed reference point, not close-ups that could be anywhere on the site. Tie the actual condition directly to the specific document that represented what was expected, by name. Boring log number. Geotech report page. Drawing note. Not "the soil report," the exact citation.

Step two: notice, in writing, inside the exact window the contract specifies. Most owners don't lose a DSC claim on the condition. They lose it because notice went out on day 12 against a 10-day clause, and once the window's closed, the merits stop mattering.

Step three: price the impact before work proceeds around it. Actual quantities and hours logged as they happen, not a lump number reconstructed from memory three weeks later when someone finally asks for it.

Here's the terrain nobody warns you about. Step two is the one that rots first. The instinct is to wait, see how bad it actually is before formalizing anything. That instinct is exactly what kills the claim. By the time the scope is fully known, the window's shut, and what's left is a story built after the fact instead of a record built in real time. A record built in real time survives a dispute. A story built afterward gets picked apart by whoever's paying it.

12% of total project budget gets lost to change orders on average, and a real share of that is a legitimate differing condition that never got documented fast enough to collect on.

Most GCs know what a differing site condition is. Very few of them have a clock running on documenting one.

Day 5 — Case Study Breakdown

A cost code with nothing behind it. That was the finding that mattered on a set where every other line item looked accounted for.

The mechanical spec called out a specialty fire suppression system for one mechanical room, fully detailed, full submittal requirements, real equipment named. The budget had no cost code for it. Somewhere between the spec being written and the estimate being finalized, that system fell into the gap between two disciplines, both of which assumed the other had priced it.

Nobody catches that by reading the spec alone, and nobody catches it by reading the budget alone. It only shows up when someone cross-references both against each other, line by line, looking for the item that exists in one and not the other.

We caught it on paper, during an independent pass, before the set went to bid. One redline, one budget revision, one line item added before a single sub ever priced the work.

Left uncaught, that system gets value-engineered out by accident, not on purpose, because it was never in a number anyone was protecting. 12% of total project budget gets lost to change orders on average, and this is exactly the kind of gap that number is made of: not a mistake anyone made on purpose, just a document nobody cross-checked.

Day 6 — Short-Form High-Signal

An indemnification clause moves who pays. It doesn't move whether the clash gets built.

Every risk allocation conversation I sit in treats the contract like it prevents the problem. It doesn't. It decides who writes the check after the problem already happened, in the field, at field-cost pricing, with the schedule already eaten.

Risk allocation is a claims strategy. It's not a QA/QC process, and treating it like one is how a well-lawyered contract still ends up sitting across the table from a change order nobody wanted to sign.

Move the review earlier. The clause only matters once something's already gone wrong.

Day 7 — Personality

I'd rather get hired by the owner's rep than the GC, and I don't think that's a popular thing to say out loud in this industry.

The GC wants the set to look clean because a clean set is a faster path to build. The owner's rep wants the set to actually be clean, because they're the one standing in the room explaining the change order to the people funding the project. Those are not the same incentive, even when everyone's being honest.

An independent review only works if the person hiring it wants the truth more than they want the schedule to hold on paper. That's the owner's rep, most of the time. Not because GCs are dishonest. Because their job is to build, and mine is to check, and those two jobs pull in different directions on the one day it actually matters.

I'll take the client who wants to know before the sub does. That's usually the person carrying the risk directly, not the person carrying the schedule.
