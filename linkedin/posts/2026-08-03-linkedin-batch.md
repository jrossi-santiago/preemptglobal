Your permit set and your for-construction set aren't the same drawings, even when everyone assumes they are.

Between permit issuance and IFC, addenda get incorporated, RFIs get resolved into revised details, and change bulletins move walls, ducts, and conduit runs. Somewhere in that stretch, the version everyone's building from stops being the version that got permitted — and almost nobody goes back and confirms the two still agree on the load-bearing decisions: rated assemblies, structural connections, life-safety egress.

Nobody decided to weaken that wall. Somewhere between two issuance rounds, a detail got redrawn and the record didn't catch up.

## Day 2 (Tue) — Nurture

Your corridor wall is carrying two different ratings at once — fire-resistance and STC — and most reviews only check one of them.

The fire rating gets checked against the life-safety code summary. The sound rating gets checked against the acoustic consultant's report. They're reviewed by different people, against different documents, and almost never cross-referenced against each other on the same wall.

The Corridor Wall Cross-Check, three passes:

Pass 1 — Assembly match. Confirm the wall type on the fire-rating schedule and the wall type on the acoustic schedule are actually the same assembly, not two different UL numbers sharing a wall tag.

Pass 2 — Continuity above ceiling. Rated and rated-for-sound walls both need to run deck-to-deck. A wall that stops at the ceiling grid satisfies neither rating, even when the drawing shows a hard line to structure.

Pass 3 — Door and frame. Confirm the door's fire label and gasketing spec actually support the STC rating the wall is supposed to deliver — a rated door with no acoustic seal breaks the sound rating even when the wall is correct.

## Day 3 (Wed) — Nurture

Your construction schedule has a need-by date for that switchgear. Has anyone checked it against how long switchgear actually takes to show up?

Procurement schedules get built off a manufacturer's quoted lead time. Construction schedules get built off when the electrical room needs to close in. Those two numbers come from different documents, drafted by different people, and the gap between them doesn't surface until submittals are already circulating.

The Long-Lead Delivery Trace, three passes:

Pass 1 — Need-by date. Pull the date from the construction schedule when the equipment has to be set and energized.

Pass 2 — Cumulative lead time. Add submittal review and approval time to the manufacturer's quoted fabrication and shipping window — not the fabrication number alone.

Pass 3 — Buffer check. Compare the two. If cumulative lead time plus a normal resubmittal cycle eats into the schedule float, that's a flag before the order gets placed, not after.

Ran this on a data center switchgear package last quarter — 26 weeks cumulative against a 20-week buffer nobody had checked. Caught before the order went in, it's a schedule adjustment. Caught after, it's an expedite fee.

## Day 5 (Fri) — Growth

A $2M renovation carries the same blind spots as an $80M build. It just gets fewer people looking for them.

Big projects get big review teams — owner's rep, CM, sometimes a second set of eyes from the lender. A rated-assembly mismatch or a missing connection detail still gets a real shot at getting caught before it ships. Smaller projects run leaner. One PM wearing three hats, an architect doing double duty as reviewer, a compressed schedule that leaves no room for a second pass on anything.

The mistake isn't more likely on a small project. It's just less likely to get caught, because the built-in redundancy that catches it on a bigger job was never staffed in the first place.

I don't scale scrutiny to project size. A 2-hour rated wall that's actually 1-hour costs the same tear-out whether it's on a $2M renovation or an $80M build — the exposure doesn't know how big the project is.

## Day 6 (Sat) — Nurture

Your elevator shaft dimensions on the structural drawings and the clearances the manufacturer's submittal actually requires aren't automatically the same number.

Structural draws the hoistway to a code-minimum shaft size early, before a manufacturer is selected. The submittal comes back later with the cab, counterweight, and rail clearances for the equipment actually being purchased — and by then the shaft walls are often already priced, sometimes already framed.

The Elevator Shaft Coordination Check, three passes:

Pass 1 — Shaft dimensions. Confirm the structural hoistway width and depth match the manufacturer's required clearances, not the code-minimum envelope the architect started from.

Pass 2 — Pit depth and overhead. Check the submittal's pit depth and overhead clearance against the structural foundation and roof framing drawings — both dimensions, not just one.

Pass 3 — Machine room and venting. Confirm the machine room size and hoistway venting shown on mechanical match what the elevator manufacturer's equipment actually requires.
