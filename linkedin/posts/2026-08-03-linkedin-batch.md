LinkedIn Posts — Week of 2026-08-03

Day 1 — Doc

Your team is building off two different versions of the same set right now, and nobody's told you yet.

Here's the fix: the Set-of-Record Check. Three questions, run before anything else happens on a project.

One: who issued the last addendum, and did every discipline receive it. Two: does the spec section match the drawing revision date, not just the sheet number. Three: is there a single owner of the master set, or is everyone working from whatever landed in their inbox last.

Miss any one of those and you've got a document control problem wearing a coordination-failure costume. It looks like a clash caught in the field. It's actually a version mismatch on paper that nobody caught first.

12% of total project budget gets lost to change orders on average. A meaningful share of that starts here, not on site.

Run the Set-of-Record Check every time a set gets released, not just at kickoff. Assign one person to own it. Put their name on it.

If you can't answer all three questions right now for your active set, stop and answer them before the next submittal goes out.

Day 2 — Story

Your punch list is longer than it needs to be, and it's not because your subs are sloppy.

I spent years on the other side of this, producing sets that later became punch list line items. Here's the pattern I kept seeing: half the items on a typical punch list aren't finish-work misses. They're clashes that were already sitting in the drawings, unresolved, waiting for someone to walk the space and notice them in person instead of on paper.

A duct that was always going to hit that beam. A wall type that never matched the fire-rating schedule three sheets over. Nobody built these wrong. Nobody caught them right either, until the punch walk.

By the time it shows up on a punch list, you're not fixing a drawing anymore. You're fixing a built space, on a schedule that's already tight, with a sub who wants to be done.

76% of projects fall behind schedule due to poor QA/QC process, and punch lists are where a lot of that delay finally becomes visible, weeks after it was actually created.

Walk your drawings as hard as you walk your space next time. The clash was there first.

Day 3 — Midas

32 days of schedule protected. $78,900 in avoided field costs. One reviewed set, before a single sub picked up a tool.

Here's how that number gets built, findings-report style: every issue in the set gets located by sheet, ranked High, Medium, or Low, and priced individually. Not a general note that coordination could be improved. A specific dollar exposure and schedule impact attached to each finding, one by one.

That's what turns a review into a number instead of an opinion. The $78,900 wasn't an estimate of the whole set's risk. It was several individual findings, each with its own price tag, added together after the fact.

Most QA/QC reports hand back a list of comments. This is a ranked report with a total at the bottom, backed by a $50K-exposure-or-free guarantee, because the exposure gets real if we're wrong.

The framework only works if it's applied to every finding, not just the obvious ones. Skip the ranking step and you're back to a punch list of opinions with no way to prioritize what gets fixed first.

Before your next set goes to build, ask for the ranked version. Sheet, severity, dollar figure. Nothing less.

Day 4 — Hot Take

Everyone treats change orders like weather. Something that just happens, budgeted for, apologized for, moved past.

That's the wrong frame, and it's costing projects 12% of total budget on average.

Here's the actual mechanism: a set gets reviewed internally only, by the same team that produced it, then it goes to build on that review alone. Everyone hopes nothing clashes. When something does clash, work stops, everyone waits on a resolution, the drawings get updated, a change order gets submitted, and the fix gets built. All of it happens at field-cost pricing instead of paper-cost pricing.

A change order isn't weather. It's the invoice for a clash that was sitting in the set the whole time, unfound, because nobody independent was hunting for it before issue.

Calling it inevitable is what lets it stay inevitable. It's not a cost of doing business. It's the cost of skipping one step: an independent pass on the set before it goes to build.

Stop budgeting for change orders like they're a law of physics. Budget for the review that prevents them instead, and watch how much of that 12% never shows up on your next project.

Day 5 — Transformation

Before: your set gets reviewed by the same team that drew it. It goes to build on that internal review alone. Everyone hopes the mechanical model and the structural model actually agree where they cross.

After: an independent pass runs the same coordination check, before the set is released, looking for exactly where those models disagree.

The before state isn't a strawman. It's the actual process on most jobs. One team checks its own work, sends it to build, and waits to find out if the hope paid off. When it doesn't, work stops in the field, everyone waits on a resolution, the drawings get updated, a change order gets submitted, and the fix gets built at field-cost pricing.

The after state catches the same clash on paper instead. Same coordination problem, same two systems fighting for the same six inches. The only thing that changed is when it got caught and who was looking.

$177 billion is lost industry-wide, every year, to uncoordinated construction items that follow this exact path from before to after and back again on the next project.

Pick which side of that line your next set lands on before it's released, not after.

Day 6 — Doc

Your submittal log is telling you there's a bottleneck. You're just not reading it that way yet.

Here's the framework: the Three-Touch Rule. Every submittal should only need three touches from issue to close. Touch one, it's logged against the spec section with a due date. Touch two, it's reviewed and stamped, no sitting in an inbox waiting for a second look. Touch three, it's returned with a clear action: approved, revise and resubmit, or rejected. Never left in limbo.

If a submittal on your log has more than three touches, that's not a difficult item. That's a process failure, and it's usually the same cause every time: nobody owns moving it to the next touch.

3 to 5 weeks of average schedule delay per commercial project comes from coordination failures like this one, where a submittal sits because ownership was never assigned, not because the item itself was complicated.

Pull your current submittal log today. Count the touches on anything still open. If you find one past touch three, assign an owner to it before end of day, not before the next meeting.

Day 7 — Vulnerability

I missed one. Early on, drawing as-builts on a project where the redlines had come back from three different subs, on three different sheet sets, none of them cross-referenced against each other.

I reconciled two of the three. I told myself the third was close enough to the design intent that it didn't need a full walk-through. It wasn't. A ceiling-mounted unit had moved from what got documented, and it stayed wrong in the O&M manual for years, past the point anyone could trace it back to that one skipped reconciliation.

Nobody caught it because nobody was independently checking my work against the field. I was confirming my own output, the same failure mode that drives the number that still bothers me most: 76% of projects fall behind schedule due to poor QA/QC process, mine included that year.

That's the lesson that stuck. Confirming your own work feels like a review. It isn't one. A review means someone else, with no stake in the original drawing, checks it against reality.

I don't skip reconciliations anymore, and I don't let anyone on our team skip them either, no matter how close enough it looks.

If your as-builts haven't been checked by someone who didn't draw them, they haven't been checked.
