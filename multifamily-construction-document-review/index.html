Why Multifamily Sets Carry More Risk

EVERY OTHER ASSET CLASS BUILDS ONE FLOOR PLAN. THIS ONE BUILDS IT DOZENS OF TIMES.

A standard construction document review checks submittals, spec-to-drawing alignment, code compliance, and MEP coordination — and a multifamily set needs every bit of that. What's different is what repetition does to a missed conflict: a wet wall that's misaligned, an electrical panel that doesn't fit, or a fire-rated assembly that's drawn wrong on the typical unit plan doesn't stay a single-unit problem. It's built into every floor that reuses that plan, on top of a podium transition and a fire-rating stack that both have to hold consistently unit to unit.

What Gets Checked On A Multifamily Set

EVERY SHEET, INCLUDING THE FLOOR YOU'RE ABOUT TO REPEAT.

The issued set is read once, in full, by reviewers who've drawn these sets themselves — not a keyword search, not a single-discipline pass. On a multifamily set, four things get checked specifically:

TYPICAL UNIT PLAN

The wet wall, electrical, and HVAC layout on the typical unit checked thoroughly — whatever's wrong there is wrong on every floor that repeats it.

VERTICAL MEP STACKING

Plumbing stacks, electrical risers, and HVAC shafts checked for alignment floor to floor through the repeated unit plan.

PODIUM TRANSITION

Structural and MEP continuity checked where wood-frame construction lands on the concrete podium below.

FIRE-RATED ASSEMBLIES & CODE

Demising wall, corridor, and shaft fire ratings checked for consistency sheet to sheet, unit to unit.

The output is a written findings report — every conflict located by sheet and detail, severity-ranked (High/Med/Low), with an estimated dollar exposure and schedule impact on each — plus a live walkthrough meeting. Before You Ask

STILL GOT QUESTIONS?
HERE'S THE STRAIGHT ANSWER.

What makes multifamily construction document review different from a standard construction document review? + –

The method is the same — an independent, owner-side check of the issued set. What's different is repetition: a typical unit plan gets built dozens or hundreds of times across a project, so a missed conflict in a wet wall, an electrical panel, or a fire-rated assembly on that plan isn't a single error — it's built into every floor that reuses it.

Why does a repeated unit plan matter so much to this kind of review? + –

Because catching a conflict once, on the typical unit plan, before it's built, prevents it from repeating on every floor that shares that plan. Catching the same conflict after three or four floors are already framed means fixing it that many times over, plus the units already built.

What's a podium transition, and why does it need its own check? + –

Many multifamily projects stack wood-frame construction on top of a concrete podium deck holding parking, retail, or amenity space. That transition is where structural systems, plumbing stacks, and electrical risers have to align across two different construction types built by different trades — a common place for a conflict to hide.

Does this replace the fire-rating and life-safety review already built into permitting? + –

No. Permit plan review confirms code compliance on its own timeline and scope. This review runs earlier, on the issued design set, reading demising walls, corridors, and shaft enclosures for the same fire-rating consistency across every repeated unit type — before a conflict reaches a plan reviewer or the field.

Who typically orders this — the owner, the developer, or the GC? + –

The owner or the owner's rep — often the developer's own capital projects or asset management team on a multifamily build. It's paid by the owner and reports to no one else. See the full breakdown built for owners →

What kinds of multifamily projects need this? + –

Garden-style, podium, and high-rise apartment or condo projects, and mixed-use residential builds with ground-floor retail or parking — any set with a repeated unit plan and multiple trades sharing tight coordination. There's no minimum construction value.
