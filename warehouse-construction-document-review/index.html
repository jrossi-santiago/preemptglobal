Why Warehouse Sets Carry More Risk

THE SLAB, THE RACKING, AND THE SPRINKLER DESIGN ALL HAVE TO AGREE. ONE CHANGE MOVES ALL THREE.

A standard construction document review checks submittals, spec-to-drawing alignment, code compliance, and MEP coordination — and a warehouse set needs every bit of that. What's layered on top is a building where the floor slab, the racking system, and the fire protection design all depend on each other — change the storage height and the sprinkler design has to follow, change the racking layout and the floor flatness spec has to hold up, all while dozens of loading docks repeat the same coordination question across the building.

Floor Flatness Tight Enough For Racking

FF/FL slab tolerances have to match what the racking or automated storage vendor actually requires, not a standard industrial floor spec.

Fire Protection Sized To What's Stored

ESFR sprinkler design and ceiling clearance depend on commodity classification and storage height — get the racking height wrong and the fire protection no longer matches.

Dozens Of Loading Docks, One Set

Dock leveler, dock door, and truck court coordination repeated across every dock position on the building.

What Gets Checked On A Warehouse Set

EVERY SHEET, INCLUDING THE ONE THE RACKING VENDOR HASN'T SEEN YET.

The issued set is read once, in full, by reviewers who've drawn these sets themselves — not a keyword search, not a single-discipline pass. On a warehouse set, four things get checked specifically:

FLOOR SLAB FLATNESS

Specified floor flatness (FF) and levelness (FL) tolerances checked against the racking or automated storage system they're actually built for.

RACKING & CLEAR-SPAN STRUCTURE

Clear-span structural dimensions and racking anchor layout cross-checked against the architectural and structural sheets.

ESFR & FIRE PROTECTION

Sprinkler design, ceiling clearance, and commodity classification checked for consistency with the racking height and storage plan actually drawn.

LOADING DOCK & TRUCK COURT

Dock leveler, dock door, and truck court coordination checked across every dock position on the building.

The output is a written findings report — every conflict located by sheet and detail, severity-ranked (High/Med/Low), with an estimated dollar exposure and schedule impact on each — plus a live walkthrough meeting. Before You Ask

STILL GOT QUESTIONS?
HERE'S THE STRAIGHT ANSWER.

What makes warehouse construction document review different from a standard construction document review? + –

The method is the same — an independent, owner-side check of the issued set. What's different is floor slab flatness tight enough for racking and automated material handling, fire protection sized to commodity classification and storage height, and structural clear spans and loading dock coordination a standard commercial set doesn't carry.

Why does floor flatness matter so much on a warehouse project? + –

Because racking and automated storage and retrieval systems are specified against a floor flatness (FF) and levelness (FL) tolerance the vendor requires to operate correctly. If the slab is drawn or poured to a looser tolerance, the racking or automated system may not perform as designed — an expensive problem to fix after the slab is placed.

How does racking height affect the fire protection design? + –

ESFR sprinkler design and ceiling clearance are sized to the commodity classification and storage height actually being racked. A change in racking height or storage type after fire protection is designed can leave the sprinkler system out of alignment with what's actually being stored — a conflict that's cheaper to catch on the issued set than after installation.

Doesn't BIM clash detection already catch this? + –

It catches a meaningful share — modeled elements overlapping in 3D. Floor flatness tolerances and fire protection commodity classifications are written requirements, not modeled geometry, so a clash report doesn't check them at all. This review reads the issued documents for exactly that. Full comparison →

When should this happen relative to a tenant's occupancy date? + –

As soon as there's an issued set — pre-bid or at IFC — since speculative and build-to-suit warehouse schedules are often locked to a tenant's committed occupancy date. Catching a conflict before the slab is poured or the racking is ordered keeps that date intact.

What kinds of warehouse projects need this? + –

Distribution centers, fulfillment centers, cold storage facilities, and warehouse or logistics builds with racking, automated material handling, or multiple loading dock positions — any set with multiple trades sharing tight coordination. There's no minimum construction value.
